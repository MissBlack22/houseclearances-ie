What Happens to Old Paperwork and Documents During a Clearance

Almost every house we clear has at least one drawer, box or filing cabinet stuffed with paperwork nobody's looked at in years. Old bank statements, utility bills, insurance renewal letters, instruction manuals for appliances long gone, school reports, the odd forgotten cheque book. Furniture and bulky items are usually the easy part of a clearance to plan for. Paperwork is different — some of it needs to be kept, some of it needs to be destroyed properly rather than just thrown out, and most of it is genuinely just clutter. Knowing which is which makes the whole job faster and safer.

What a Clearance Team Actually Does With It

A clearance crew isn't there to read your post, and a reputable one won't treat paperwork the same as everything else going into the van. The standard approach is to set anything that looks official, financial or personal to one side rather than clearing it in with general household waste — things like bank or building society correspondence, anything with an account number, a signature, or a date of birth on it. Obvious non-personal paper — old newspapers, takeaway menus, flyers, instruction leaflets — goes straight into general recycling. If you'd rather paperwork wasn't handled at all during the clearance, say so when you book: it's easy to agree that anything from a particular drawer, cabinet or room is left completely untouched for you to go through separately.

Worth Sorting Yourself First

Where possible, it's worth going through filing cabinets, document boxes and bedside drawers yourself before the clearance date, even just a quick pass. Nobody knows which envelope matters more than the person who lived there, and a few minutes spent checking drawers and box files removes any doubt about something important ending up in a skip. This matters even more during a bereavement clearance, when a will, a pension book, share certificates or a bank book can turn up somewhere unexpected. What's Usually Worth Keeping

A few categories are worth holding onto rather than clearing out on a whim:

  • Tax and financial records. Revenue generally expects taxpayers and businesses to retain records relevant to a tax return for six years, so don't clear out recent receipts, invoices or self-assessment paperwork on the assumption they're no longer needed.
  • Property documents. Deeds, mortgage paperwork, planning permission letters and service records for the house itself — these are usually held by a solicitor or bank rather than loose in the house, but check before assuming a copy isn't needed.
  • Certificates. Birth, marriage, death and education certificates are awkward and sometimes slow to replace, so these are worth setting aside even if nothing else in the drawer is.
  • Insurance and pension paperwork. Policy numbers and provider details save a lot of phone calls later if a claim or query ever comes up.

Why Sensitive Paperwork Shouldn't Just Go in the Bin

Bank statements, payslips, medical correspondence and anything carrying a PPS number or account details shouldn't go into general waste or an ordinary recycling bin whole. That kind of paperwork is exactly what's targeted in identity fraud and theft from bins, and a full name and address alongside financial details is often all that's needed. The safer route is proper shredding or confidential destruction rather than tearing a few pages up by hand — several Irish shredding providers collect and securely destroy bulk paperwork, and some run periodic free shredding days through local councils. If a document doesn't need keeping and has any personal or financial detail on it, treat it as something to shred, not recycle directly.

Paper That Can Just Go for Recycling

Once anything personal or official has been set aside, the bulk of what's left in most houses is genuinely just paper: old magazines, catalogues, non-personal correspondence, packaging inserts, expired takeaway menus. None of that needs special handling — it goes into ordinary paper recycling along with the rest of a clearance's recyclable waste. The distinction that matters isn't paper versus non-paper, it's whether a page has someone's personal details on it.

Paperwork Found During a Bereavement Clearance

Clearing a family member's home after a death is when paperwork tends to carry the most weight, and the most risk of something being missed in a rushed clearance. Executors are often working through a house at the same time as probate, bank notifications and Revenue correspondence, which makes it worth going through paperwork in stages rather than all at once on clearance day.
