DIY House Clearance vs Hiring a Professional: What It Actually Costs You

Clearing a house yourself feels like the obvious way to save money. Hire a skip, borrow or rent a van, make a few trips to the recycling centre, and skip the clearance company's fee entirely. For a single room or a light job, that can genuinely be the cheaper route. For a full house, the maths is a lot less one-sided than it looks before you start.

What a DIY Clearance Actually Involves

A full DIY clearance is rarely just one task. It usually means hiring a skip for what can go straight to landfill or mixed recycling, hiring or borrowing a van for anything too big or awkward for a skip, making repeat trips to a council recycling centre for items the skip can't take, separately arranging collection for electricals under WEEE rules (see our guide to what actually happens to old electronics during a clearance), and finding somewhere for anything you'd rather donate or sell than throw out. Each of those is its own logistics problem, with its own cost.

The Costs That Are Easy to Add Up

Skip hire in Ireland is usually quoted by size, with a mini skip costing noticeably less than a builder's or midi skip, and hire periods that vary by company — some give you a few days, others closer to a week, with extra charges if you need to keep it longer. Van hire for a day adds another cost on top, and prices vary a lot by company, van size and whether you're covered for damage excess. Both are worth getting an actual quote for rather than assuming a figure, since rates move and differ between operators.

The Costs People Forget to Add

This is where the DIY total usually grows. Council recycling centres and civic amenity sites are generally set up for householders disposing of their own domestic waste, and most explicitly do not accept commercial waste — so if you're clearing a property as part of any kind of paid arrangement, a standard household drop-off may not be the right route at all. Many centres also charge per load or per item for bulky waste rather than letting everything in for free, and those charges have been rising at several Dublin-area councils, so check the current fee on your own council's site before you plan around it. On top of that, a full house rarely fits in one skip and one van load — it's usually several trips, each one costing fuel, parking and a chunk of a weekend, which is time most people are also trying to spend on everything else involved in a move, a sale or a bereavement.

What You're Actually Paying For With a Professional

A professional clearance quote isn't just "someone else does the lifting." It covers licensed disposal through the correct waste routes for every category of item, a crew sized and equipped for heavy or awkward pieces like wardrobes, sofas and appliances, one visit instead of a string of separate trips, and someone else taking on the manual handling risk of moving heavy furniture down stairs or through tight doorways — not something to underestimate when it's a fridge or a wardrobe on a narrow staircase. It also means items in good condition can be set aside for reuse rather than binned by default. One Thing That Catches People Out: Permits

If you're clearing your own house, moving your own belongings doesn't require a waste collection permit. It's a different story if someone does it for you as a paid job or on a commercial basis — collecting and moving waste that way is regulated, and doing it without the right permit from the National Waste Collection Permit Office is an offence. If a "man with a van" quote looks unusually cheap, it's worth asking whether they're actually permitted to collect and dispose of waste at all. Our explainer on what a waste collection permit actually means covers this in more detail, including how to check a company's permit yourself.

Where DIY Genuinely Makes Sense

  • A single room or a small volume that fits in one car boot trip or a single skip, with no awkward access.
  • You have the time to spare across several weekends and don't mind the repeat trips to a recycling centre.
  • Nothing heavy or awkward is involved — no wardrobes that don't come apart, no appliances, no stairs to navigate.
  • You're happy to sort disposal routes yourself, including checking which items your council's recycling centre will and won't take.

Where DIY usually stops making sense is a full house, a property with difficult access, a short deadline such as a sale closing or a tenancy ending, or any situation where you simply don't have a spare few weekends to give to it.
