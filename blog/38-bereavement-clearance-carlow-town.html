Bereavement Clearance in Carlow Town: What to Expect From First Call to Empty House

If you are reading this, someone close to you has probably died, and you have been left with their home. Perhaps it is a house you grew up in. Perhaps it is a place you visited every Sunday. Either way, the practical question has arrived: what happens to everything in it, and who is going to do it?

This guide is about what to expect if you ask us to help with a bereavement clearance in Carlow Town. It follows the job from the first conversation to the keys being handed back, explains what we need from you and what you can expect from us, and flags the decisions that are worth making before the day. It is not legal advice, and we are not a substitute for a solicitor. It is written to make the unfamiliar a little more familiar.

You Do Not Have to Be Ready

Many people put off the first call because they feel they should have a plan before they ring. You do not need one. You do not need to have sorted anything, decided on a date, or even decided whether to go ahead. A first conversation is a chance to ask questions and to hear how the job would work. There is no obligation, and nobody will press you.

If you are not ready to speak, send a message instead. Many families prefer WhatsApp for exactly this reason: you can write at your own pace, attach photographs when you are able, and read the reply when it suits you.

The First Conversation: What We Will Ask

When you get in touch, the questions are simple and practical. We ask them so that the quote is accurate and the job runs smoothly, not to pry.

  • Where is the property? The town or area, and the type of house or apartment. We cover Carlow Town and the surrounding county; see the areas we cover for the list.
  • How big is it? Roughly how many bedrooms, and whether it is furnished throughout.
  • Is there an attic, garage, shed or storage? These can be a large part of the job and are easy to forget.
  • What is the access like? Stairs, steps, narrow hallways, a long path, parking.
  • Is there a date you are working to? A sale, a rental handback, a funeral month's-mind, a family visit, or no date at all.
  • Who will be at the house? Who holds the key, and who makes decisions on the day.
  • Is there anything that must be kept, or anything you are worried about? This is the most important question, and we will come back to it.

You can answer as much or as little as you like. We will ask again when we need to.

The Decision That Matters Most: Who Decides

Before the day, someone needs to be the person who decides. In most families this is the executor named in the will, or the next of kin where there is no will. In practice it is often the sibling who lives closest.

If more than one person is involved, agree a few things before the clearance, and write them down, even in a short email:

  1. who speaks to the clearance company and has authority to say yes
  2. who holds the key and will be at the house, or who will do a video walk-through
  3. what each person wants to see or take before anything goes
  4. a cut-off date for taking things

If anything about the will, the executor or the estate is unclear or disputed, speak to a solicitor before anything of value leaves the house.

Probate, Timing and the Question Everyone Asks

The question we are asked most often is: can we clear the house before probate has been granted? The honest answer is that it depends on the estate, and the solicitor handling it should advise. In general, families can often arrange a clearance while probate is in progress, provided that nothing of value is disposed of without a record, and that the executor and any other beneficiaries are aware. Our guide on clearing a house before probate lays out the questions worth asking.

On timelines, our executor's checklist notes that a grant of probate can take months, and that the Probate Office that handles an application depends on where the deceased lived. If you are not sure which applies to a Carlow address, ask the solicitor, or check the Courts Service guidance. Planning your clearance around probate is therefore a matter of asking, not guessing.

Sometimes the house cannot be left empty for long, for example when it is rented, when it is costing money to heat and insure, or when a sale is waiting. Sometimes there is no hurry at all. Whichever applies, tell us at the start, because it affects how we schedule the work.

Valuables, Papers and Anything That Might Be Worth Something

A house that has been lived in for decades often holds more than it appears to. Before any clearance, it is worth a deliberate search for:

  • Documents: the will, deeds, insurance papers, pension and bank records, passports, birth, marriage and death certificates, vehicle papers, tax documents
  • Cash and valuables: envelopes, jewellery, watches, coins, medals
  • Collections: stamps, coins, records, books, antiques, paintings, clocks
  • Photographs, letters and diaries: the things you cannot replace
  • Keys and codes: for sheds, safes, cars, alarms, post boxes
  • Electronic devices: phones, tablets, laptops, hard drives

Look in the places people store things: coat pockets, handbags, the top of wardrobes, under mattresses, inside biscuit tins and books, the back of drawers, and the attic. Take your time. It is much easier to find something before a clearance than afterwards.

Valuations

If you think something might be valuable, such as an antique, a collection or a painting, get advice before it goes. An auctioneer, valuer or specialist dealer can tell you, and if the estate is involved, the solicitor should know. We are a clearance company, not valuers, and we do not value your belongings. What we do is set aside anything that looks personal or valuable, so that it is not discarded by mistake.

Things we will not take without asking

Some items need a decision from the family first: documents, photographs, jewellery, cash, medication, and anything with sentimental value. If a crew member finds something of this kind, it is put aside, and we ask you what you want done with it.

Older Town Houses and Newer Estates

Carlow Town has an older centre with terraced and townhouse-style properties, and newer estates around it. They raise different practical points.

Older town-centre properties

  • Narrow hallways and steep stairs mean more carrying, and long items such as wardrobes may need to be dismantled.
  • Parking may be on the street, so tell us the street name and whether there are restrictions.
  • Attics are often reached by a small hatch and a loft ladder, which limits what can be carried out safely.
  • Houses that have been in one family a long time may have accumulated more than a modern house.

Newer estates

  • A driveway usually means quick loading.
  • Garages are often full, and the attic may be boarded, so volume is the main factor.
  • Open-plan layouts and fitted furniture are usually quicker to clear than a house with many small rooms.

Apartments

  • Tell the management company in advance.
  • Ask about lift size, loading arrangements and any restricted hours.
  • Our apartment clearance guide lists the questions to ask, and it applies to any building.

The Clearance Day

On the day, the crew arrives at the agreed time. Here is what to expect.

  1. A short walk-through. We meet you or the key-holder, confirm which rooms are in scope, what is staying and what has been set aside, and look at any access issues together.
  2. Protecting the house. Floors, hallways and banisters are protected where needed.
  3. Furniture first. Larger items go first so that the rooms open up. Items that will not fit through doors are dismantled.
  4. Contents, room by room. Clothes, kitchenware, books and general belongings are sorted as they go: reusable, recyclable or waste.
  5. Attic, garage and shed. Cleared alongside or after the main house.
  6. A sweep. Loose debris is swept up.
  7. A final walk-through. We go through each room and outbuilding with you or the key-holder, and agree that the job is complete.

Most single-property clearances are completed in one visit. A large house with a full attic, garage and shed can take two days. We will tell you what to expect when we quote.

You do not have to be there. Some people prefer to be in the house for the whole day, some arrive for the final walk-through, and some prefer to be in another place entirely. All three are fine.

What Happens to Everything

It is reasonable to ask where a loved one's belongings are going. In a responsible clearance, items follow an order of priority:

  1. Kept by the family. Everything you choose to take.
  2. Reuse. Furniture, household goods, books and clothes in usable condition are set aside for donation or resale where that is realistic.
  3. Recycling. Metal, electricals, cardboard, textiles and other materials go to recycling routes.
  4. Disposal. Only what cannot be reused or recycled is disposed of, through licensed facilities.

We aim for an average recycling rate of up to 95% across the clearances we carry out. For the detail, see what happens to your belongings after a clearance.

Anyone who takes waste from a house should hold a waste collection permit, because a householder can be held responsible if it is dumped. Items That Need Special Handling

  • Medication. Return unused or out-of-date medication to a pharmacy.
  • Paint, chemicals, fuel and gas bottles. Handled separately from general waste. Tell us what is there. See household versus hazardous waste.
  • Heating oil. If the house has an oil tank, ask the oil supplier or a qualified person about its contents and what to do before the house is sold or left. A tank is a fixture and is not something the clearance crew will remove as part of a normal house clearance.
  • Electrical items. Fridges, freezers, cookers, televisions and small electricals are recycled through electrical waste routes.
  • Anything unusual. If you find something you would not expect to find in a house, do not move it. Ask for advice and, if necessary, contact the Gardaí.
  • Personal data. Remove or secure papers and devices that contain personal or financial details.

Afterwards: The Weeks After the Clearance

When the crew leaves, the house is empty and swept. The next steps depend on what comes next:

  • Take dated photographs of each room, for the estate and for your records.
  • Return or collect the keys, and change the locks or alarm codes if you are selling or letting.
  • Read the meters and tell utility providers about the change.
  • Arrange cleaning if the house is going on the market or being let.
  • Update the insurer if the house is empty.
  • Keep the paperwork from the clearance, including the quote and any receipts, with the estate papers.

Many people say that the weeks after a clearance are harder than the days before it. The house is empty, the practical work has stopped, and the loss feels more real. That is common. It is a reason to be kind to yourself, rather than a sign that something has gone wrong.

A Room-by-Room Walk-Through Before the Day

If you can visit once before the clearance, a slow walk through the house with a notebook is worth more than any amount of worrying at a distance. This is a way to do it, room by room.

Hall and landing

Coats, shoes, umbrellas and the small table by the door often hold keys, letters and loose change. Check the pockets of coats before they are bagged. Look at what is on the walls, because pictures and mirrors are easy to forget.

Living room

Look at display cabinets, shelves and the contents of sideboard drawers. This is where family photographs, ornaments, china and papers tend to collect. Check the backs and undersides of furniture for envelopes and notes.

Kitchen

Crockery, cutlery, pots and appliances are easy to donate but slow to sort. Look in the presses above the cooker, the back of the cupboard under the sink and any biscuit tins. Empty the fridge, freezer and food presses before the clearance.

Bedrooms

Wardrobes, chests of drawers and bedside lockers hold clothing, jewellery, medication and personal papers. Check under the bed and the top of the wardrobe. Mattresses and bedding go with the clearance unless you say otherwise.

Bathroom

Medication, toiletries and cleaning products. Medication goes to a pharmacy; cleaning chemicals are handled as household waste.

Attic, garage and shed

These often hold the most volume and the least sorted belongings. Look for suitcases, boxes marked with names, tools, bikes, Christmas decorations and paint. Our post on what is usually worth keeping from an attic helps with the decisions.

Outside

Garden furniture, pots, tools and the contents of any outbuildings. If the garden is overgrown, say so, as green waste adds volume.

Make a note of anything that you are unsure about, with a photograph, and send it to us. A question asked before the day is easier to answer than a problem found on it.

Different Circumstances, Different Considerations

The person had been in a nursing home or hospital

A house that has been empty for months may need airing, and may have stored items that were not touched for a long time. Practical matters such as insurance and security matter more. Allow extra time for sorting, because belongings may have been moved around by family.

The person rented their home

Check the lease and talk to the landlord about notice and handover as early as possible. Dated photographs of the empty property are especially important. Our guide to end of tenancy clearance explains how deposits and deductions work.

The home is shared with someone who is still living there

If a spouse, partner or other relative still lives in the house, the clearance may be partial and the pace theirs. We can clear a single room, such as a bedroom, or particular items, and leave the rest.

The person ran a small business from the house

Stock, equipment and paperwork may need separate handling, and some records must be kept. Our guide to clearing a deceased relative's business or office covers it.
