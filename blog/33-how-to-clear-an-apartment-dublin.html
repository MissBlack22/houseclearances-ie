How to Clear an Apartment in Dublin: Building Rules, Timing and a Step-by-Step Plan

Clearing a flat is a different job from clearing a house, and the difference is rarely the amount of stuff. A one-bedroom apartment holds far less than a family home, but it usually has to leave through a shared hallway, a lift or a narrow stairwell, past neighbours, and onto a street where nobody is guaranteed a parking space. Those shared parts of the building, not the contents of the flat, are what most often turn a simple job into a stressful one.

This guide is for anyone facing that job in Dublin: a tenant handing back keys, a landlord turning a unit around, an owner who is downsizing or selling, or an executor or family member clearing a flat after a death. It sets out the order to do things in, what to check with the building before anything is moved, and where a clearance company helps and where you can manage perfectly well yourself. If you would rather have it done for you, our apartment clearance service covers exactly this.

The one thing to do first: find out how the building lets things in and out. Ten minutes on the phone to the management company, agent or caretaker, before you book anyone, prevents most of the problems described below.

Step 1: Work Out Which Situation You Are In

The right plan depends on who is clearing and why, because each situation has a different deadline and a different person who decides what happens to the contents.

  • A tenant moving out. Your deadline is the end of the tenancy and the condition the landlord expects the flat to be returned in. Everything that is yours needs to go; anything that belongs to the landlord stays. The Residential Tenancies Board explains the difference between normal wear and tear and damage on its wear and tear page, and deposit questions are covered on its security deposits pages.
  • A landlord or letting agent. The deadline is the next tenancy, and every empty day costs rent. The questions are what a previous tenant has left behind, who is responsible for removing it, and how fast the unit can be ready to photograph and re-let. Our guide on end of tenancy clearance and deposit deductions and the landlord clearance checklist go into this.
  • An owner downsizing or selling. You have the most control over timing and the most decisions to make: what moves with you, what is sold or donated, and what goes. Start earlier than you think you need to. Our downsizing guide helps with those decisions.
  • An executor or family after a death. The pace is yours to set, and the legal position matters before anything of value is disposed of. Read our guide for families and, if you are the executor, can you clear a house before probate. This is general information; the solicitor handling the estate is the right person to confirm the legal side.
  • A flat with a very large amount of belongings. If the unit is crowded floor to ceiling, the building's constraints combine with the volume. See our non-judgemental guide to hoarder clearance; the approach is slower and more careful.

Step 2: Find Out the Building's Rules

Many Dublin apartment blocks are run by an owners' management company, which is responsible for the shared parts of the building: entrance halls, landings, lifts and staircases, along with refuse arrangements and the rules residents are asked to follow. Citizens Information explains the role of these companies and how their house rules work. In practice, the building may have rules about moving or removing large items that nobody mentions until the day. Older buildings, such as a house split into flats, may have no formal rules at all but have their own practical limits.

Ask the management company, managing agent or caretaker these questions, and write the answers down:

  • Is there a lift, and what size is it? Is there a separate goods or service lift?
  • Does moving or removing furniture have to be booked in advance, and for which hours?
  • Is there a loading bay, a set-down area or a visitor space that can be reserved?
  • Do contractors need to provide proof of insurance or a waste permit before they can work in the common areas?
  • Are there rules about protecting hallways, lift interiors or stairwells (floor covering, wall protection)?
  • Is a deposit or fee charged for using the lift for a move or clearance?
  • Where do bins and bulky items go, and are residents allowed to leave anything in the bin store?
  • Is there a basement store, bike store or storage locker that belongs to this unit?

Do not assume the answers. A lift that comfortably takes two people and a sofa in one building may be too small for a wardrobe in the next, and a block that allows removals at any hour on Saturday may forbid them on Sunday. A clearance company will ask the same questions, and having the answers ready speeds up the quote.

Step 3: Think About the Journey Out

The mental picture to have in your head is the route the biggest items must take: from the room, through the flat's own doorways and hall, to the lift or stairs, through the entrance, to wherever the vehicle can stop. The tightest point on that route decides what can be moved whole and what has to be taken apart.

Stairs and no lift

Period conversions, such as a Georgian or Victorian house split into flats in Rathmines or Portobello, often mean narrow turning stairs and small landings, with no lift. Heavy furniture can usually still come out, but it takes more trips, more people and sometimes dismantling. Tell whoever is quoting which floor the flat is on and how many flights there are.

Lifts

Modern blocks usually have a passenger lift, and sometimes a larger service lift. Where there is a distinction, the building will often expect bulky items to use the service lift. Booking windows are common. Find out whether you are allowed to hold the lift, and for how long, so the crew is not waiting.

Parking and loading

Dublin parking is its own problem. Resident permit schemes, pay-and-display zones and single allocated bays all affect how close a van can get to the door, and the distance from van to flat decides how long the job takes. Ask the management company whether a bay can be reserved, and where relevant check with your local authority about any temporary arrangements for the street. Do not assume a clearance vehicle can park for hours outside a block simply because you need it to.

Step 4: Sort Before You Clear

The cheapest, calmest clearances are the ones where the decisions were made first. Work in this order:

  1. Set aside what is staying or going with you. Valuables, documents, keepsakes, anything you or the family want to keep. If you are an executor, check with the solicitor before moving or disposing of anything of value.
  2. Identify what belongs to the landlord or to the building. In a furnished rental, the landlord's furniture and appliances stay. Photograph them and the condition they are in. In an owned flat, check what is included in a sale with the estate agent or solicitor, such as fitted wardrobes, appliances, light fittings and curtains.
  3. Sell or give away what has value. Furniture in good condition, working appliances and anything with a resale market can be sold, listed for collection or donated. This is worth doing early, because collection windows take time to arrange.
  4. Donate what a charity can use. Charity shops differ in what they accept, so check before turning up. The SVP list of shops that accept furniture is a useful starting point.
  5. Separate what needs special handling. We cannot take paint, solvents, oils or chemicals. Gas bottles, batteries and electrical items are not general waste either. Our guide to household versus hazardous waste explains which is which.
  6. Leave the rest for the clearance. Bulky furniture, mattresses, broken items, and the general accumulation of a lived-in home.

For something to tick off as you go, use the apartment clearance checklist, which can be printed or saved as a PDF.

Step 5: Deal With the Awkward Items

Most of a flat is straightforward. A handful of things cause nearly all the difficulty:

  • Mattresses and sofas. They are bulky rather than heavy, and large sofas can fail to turn corners on stairs. Measure the route before deciding whether a sofa comes out whole.
  • Wardrobes and bed frames. Many can be dismantled with basic tools, which makes them easier to carry and less likely to mark walls.
  • Fridge-freezers, washing machines and cookers. Heavy, and electrical items have their own recycling rules. Empty and defrost fridges and freezers beforehand, and disconnect appliances if you can do so safely.
  • Fitted items. Built-in wardrobes, kitchens and appliances usually stay with the property. Do not remove them unless you have been told to.
  • Balcony and storage contents. It is common to forget the balcony, the bike store and the storage locker. Include them in the plan and in your photos.

Dublin City Council runs a bulky household waste collection for households in its area, for a nominal fee, which can suit one or two large items. Check the council's current terms and whether your building is eligible. For a whole flat, or anything the service does not take, a permitted clearance company is usually quicker.

Step 6: Decide Whether to Do It Yourself, Hire a Van or Hire a Clearance Company

All three are reasonable, depending on the size of the job and your time:

  • Do it yourself. Sensible when there is little to remove, you have a vehicle and help, and the building allows it. It is slower and harder than people expect once you add the lift, parking and a trip to a recycling centre or waste facility.
  • Hire a man and a van. Fine for moving items, but you need to check whether they are authorised to take waste. We cover the difference in our guide to man and van versus a clearance company.
  • Hire a clearance company. Sensible when the flat is fully furnished, the deadline is tight, you are not in Dublin, or there is more than you can sort and carry. The company should hold a valid waste collection permit. Our guides on how to choose a house clearance company and on what a waste collection permit actually means explain how to check.

The quickest route to a firm figure is photographs. Take a short run of clear photos or a video of every room, the hallway, the balcony and any store, and add a few facts: the floor, whether there is a lift, where the vehicle can stop, and the date you need it done by. What Happens on Clearance Day

  1. The crew arrives at the agreed time, and the vehicle is positioned in the arranged bay or set-down area.
  2. Lift and common areas are protected if the building requires it.
  3. Items are carried out room by room, with anything you set aside left alone.
  4. Furniture is dismantled where needed to get it through doors and stairwells.
  5. Reusable items are separated where possible, and the rest is loaded for recycling or disposal.
  6. The flat is left empty, and you or the landlord walk through to confirm nothing has been missed.
  7. Keys are returned to whoever is expecting them.

You do not need to be there. Many tenants, landlords, agents and executors arrange access with a caretaker or key-holder and let the crew get on with it. If you would rather be present, that is fine too.

Common Mistakes

  • Booking the clearance before checking the building's rules. A lift that cannot be used, or a bay that was never reserved, costs time on the day.
  • Leaving the storage locker or balcony out of the quote. It is the most common surprise.
  • Clearing before deciding what belongs to whom. This matters especially in furnished lets and in estates.
  • Leaving it to the last 48 hours. Lifts, bays and crews all need notice, and rushed clearances cost more in stress even where they do not cost more in money.
  • Not photographing the finished flat. Take photos after the clearance, for your records, particularly if a deposit or handover depends on the condition of the unit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to tell the management company before an apartment clearance?

Usually, yes. Many buildings have rules about moving or removing large items, lift booking and loading, and some management companies ask contractors for proof of insurance and a waste permit. Check with them before you book a date.

Can you clear an apartment with no lift?

Yes. It takes more trips and sometimes dismantling furniture, so tell whoever is quoting which floor the flat is on and how many flights of stairs there are.

Do I need to be there on the day?

No. If access is arranged through a caretaker, agent or key-holder, the crew can get on with it. You are welcome to be there if you prefer.

What if the building has a loading bay I have to book?

Book it as soon as you have a date, and tell the clearance company the booking window so the crew arrives inside it. If it can be reserved on your behalf, ask the management company.

Can you clear just one room, or a storage locker?

Yes. Partial clearances of a single room, a balcony or a locker are quoted in the same way, usually from photographs.

How do I get a quote if I am not in Ireland?

Send photos and a short video of every room, plus the floor number, lift details and the date you need it done. Access can be arranged through a relative, agent or solicitor.
