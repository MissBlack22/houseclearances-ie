Clearing an Empty House Over the Winter: Heating, Damp, Short Days and the Order of Work

A house that becomes empty in November, December or January has a harder few months ahead of it than one that empties in May. Nobody is opening windows, running taps or putting the heating on, and the house sits closed and cold while the family deals with the funeral and the solicitor. Executors who inherit a house at this time of year often plan to sell or let it in the spring, which is sensible, but the house has to come through a winter first.

This guide is about getting it through: what cold and damp do to an unheated home, what to tell the insurer, how to keep a little heat and air moving, why the soft furnishings and perishables should leave first, how the short daylight affects photographs, viewings and the clearance, and what can wait until spring. It is general practical guidance, not legal or insurance advice; the solicitor and your insurer confirm the specifics. What a Winter Does to an Unheated House

Three things happen in a closed, cold house, and all get worse the longer it is left.

  • Damp and condensation. Cold walls and still air mean moisture settles on windows, in corners and behind furniture. Within weeks there can be a musty smell; within months, mould on mattresses, upholstered chairs, curtains, clothes in wardrobes, books and papers. Soft things go first, and once mouldy they are not reusable.
  • Frozen pipes. In a cold snap, water in an unheated house can freeze and split a pipe, and the damage comes when it thaws and a tank or pipe empties through a ceiling. It is one of the worst things that can happen to an empty house, and it is largely preventable.
  • Neglect that shows. Post piles up behind the door, leaves block the gutters and the house begins to look unoccupied. A neighbour who keeps an eye on it is worth more than any gadget.

Pests also move indoors for warmth. If the house has already stood empty for years, our guide to clearing a long-vacant house covers the safety checks that apply.

The First Week: Insurer, Heat, Water and Air

Tell the insurer

Home insurance policies commonly have conditions about unoccupied properties, and those conditions can affect a claim if a pipe does burst. We cannot tell you what your policy says. Ring the insurer, explain that the house is empty and why, ask what the policy requires over the winter, and write down the answer and the date. If the house belongs to an estate, tell the solicitor.

Decide about heat and water

There are two sensible approaches, and the house, the heating system and the insurer decide between them. One is to leave the heating on a low setting or a timer so the house never gets cold enough for pipes to freeze. The other is to turn the water off at the stopcock and have the system drained down so there is nothing to freeze. Ask a plumber which suits the house; do not simply turn everything off and hope. Either way the electricity should stay on, for the photographs, the viewings and the clearance.

Let air move

Open the internal doors, leave wardrobe and press doors ajar, pull back the curtains and open the trickle vents if there are any. On visits, open the windows while you are there. Empty the fridge and freezer, take out the bins and redirect or collect the post.

Why the Soft Furnishings and Perishables Go First

Most families plan a single clearance for when the solicitor gives the go-ahead, which may be months away. That is fine for the furniture, the kitchen and the garage, but not for the things damp gets to first. If the house will stand cold until spring, the first visit should deal with the following.

  • Food in the fridge, freezer and presses, and anything open in the bathroom.
  • Bedding and mattresses, which hold moisture and smell before anything else does.
  • Clothes and towels in wardrobes, drawers and the hot press; if the family wants to go through them, do it now.
  • Papers and photographs, which damp ruins quietly. Gather them into one box and take them to the solicitor or home; our guide to old paperwork and documents during a clearance explains what to keep.
  • Upholstered chairs, cushions, rugs and curtains that are already damp.

What may be removed before probate is a question for the solicitor; our guide on clearing a house before probate sets out the general position, which is that securing and tidying are fine and anything of significance waits. Perishables and damp bedding are rarely the problem; if in doubt, photograph them first. A partial clearance of this kind is a normal first step; see full versus partial house clearance.

Short Days: Photographs, Viewings and the Crew

In midwinter the light is going by mid-afternoon, and that affects three things. The estate agent's photographs need daylight, so if the house is to be marketed before spring the photographer wants a bright late morning, curtains open and lights working. Viewings are squeezed into the same hours, and a cold, dark house shows badly; a little heat and every bulb working make more difference than anything else. And the crew works a full day from 8am, but a garden or shed without light is slow going, so outside spaces are planned for the middle of the day.

Planning a Clearance Day in Winter

A winter clearance is a normal booking, but a few things matter that would not in June.

  • Access and the ground. Wet leaves, ice on a path and a soft lawn slow the carrying. Tell us where the truck can stand and how far it is to the door.
  • Light and power. Leave the electricity on. If a room or the attic has no working light, say so in advance so the crew can plan for it.
  • Floors. Wet boots and many trips in and out are hard on carpets and timber. The crew protects floors and doorways on the route out.
  • The fridge and freezer. Emptied and defrosted the day before if they are going.
  • Hazardous items. Paint, solvents, oils, fuel, chemicals, batteries and gas cylinders cannot come with us; see household versus hazardous waste.
  • Keys and the alarm. Decide who hands over the keys, how the crew gets the alarm code, and who gets the keys back.
  • Daylight for the walk-through. The final walk-through is easier in daylight, so an 8am start suits winter.

What Can Wait Until Spring

Some things are better left until the weather turns.

  • The garden. Cutting back and tidying are easier and look better done in spring, just before the photographs. Our guide to garden clearance before selling explains what is worth doing, and garden waste clearance can be a separate visit.
  • Outside repairs and painting. Clear the gutters of leaves now, because blocked gutters can cause damp; everything else outside waits for dry weather.
  • The sale itself. If the agent suggests waiting for spring, the winter is for getting the house empty, dry and warm enough to show well when they say go. The Greystones timeline sets out the months from the funeral to the sale.

What should not wait: the perishables, the bedding and soft furnishings, the papers and photographs, the insurer, and the decision about heat and water.

A Winter Checklist for an Empty House

  1. Ring the insurer about unoccupied conditions; note the answer.
  2. Ask a plumber: low constant heat, or water off and the system drained.
  3. Leave the electricity on; check the lights work.
  4. Empty the fridge, freezer and presses; take out the bins; redirect the post.
  5. Open internal doors, wardrobes and vents; open the curtains.
  6. Remove bedding, mattresses, damp textiles, papers and photographs on the first visit, with the solicitor's agreement.
  7. Clear the gutters of leaves.
  8. Ask a neighbour to keep an eye on the house and hold a key.
  9. Walk through on every visit: damp patches, drips, the boiler, signs of mice.
  10. Book the full clearance for when the solicitor allows, with an early start.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should we leave the heating on in an empty house over the winter?

Either keep a low, constant heat so the pipes cannot freeze, or turn the water off and have the system drained down. A plumber can say which suits the house, and the insurer may have a view. Do not simply switch everything off and leave it.

Can we remove bedding, food and clothes before probate is granted?

Ask the solicitor handling the estate, but perishables and damp textiles are rarely the problem; securing and tidying are generally fine, and anything of significance waits. Photograph things before they go if unsure.

Do you clear houses in winter weather?

Yes. Rain does not stop a clearance, and we work Monday to Saturday, 8am to 5pm, through the winter. Ice on a steep path may change the plan, and outside spaces are done while there is light.

What should go first if the house will stand empty until spring?

Food, bedding and mattresses, clothes and towels, papers and photographs, and any upholstery or rugs already damp. These are what cold and damp ruin first. Furniture, the kitchen and the garage can wait for the main clearance.

Can you clear the house if we live abroad and nobody can travel in winter?

Yes. A neighbour, the agent or the solicitor hands over the key, the crew confirms the scope with you by video, works from your labels, sends photographs of every empty room and returns the keys as agreed.

Will furniture that has been in a cold, damp house be reused?

Solid wood furniture usually survives a winter and is set aside for reuse where realistic. Mattresses, upholstery and anything musty or mouldy are disposed of rather than donated.

Should we clear the garden in winter?

Clear the gutters now, because blocked gutters can cause damp, and leave the rest until spring, when the work is easier and the result lasts until the photographs.
