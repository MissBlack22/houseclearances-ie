A Landlord's Guide to Clearing a Commercial Unit Before a Lease Ends

A tenant's lease is ending, and the unit is still full — shelving, old stock, a counter that was never taken down, a skip of rubbish nobody got around to booking. Whether the tenant has already gone or is on the way out, the unit usually has to be cleared before it can be shown to a new tenant, and it's worth knowing where that job actually sits before you book anyone to do it.

Check the Lease Before You Book Anything

Most commercial leases in Ireland put a "yield up" obligation on the tenant: handing the unit back with vacant possession, generally restored to the condition it was in at the start of the lease, unless a schedule of condition attached to the lease says otherwise. That covenant commonly extends to removing fit-out and alterations the tenant made during the term, not just taking their own belongings away. If a schedule of condition exists, check it — it can limit what the tenant actually has to put right. Where a break option is involved, it may be conditional on the tenant complying with the yield-up clause in full, which is worth knowing before you agree anything informally about leaving items behind.

In practice, this means the lease — not goodwill — decides who pays to clear what's left. If the tenant is cooperative and still contactable, it's worth raising the yield-up clause with them directly before arranging a clearance yourself, since a tenant who clears the unit themselves (or pays for it) may save you the cost and the argument. If the tenant has disappeared, gone into liquidation, or simply isn't responding, you may be left clearing it yourself to get the unit back on the market, with the cost potentially recoverable from a deposit, guarantee or through a dilapidations claim depending on the lease terms.

What's Usually Left Behind

Commercial units tend to leave a different mix than houses. Retail units often have shelving, display units, signage, security shutters' internals, and point-of-sale fittings. Offices leave desks, partitioning, filing cabinets and server racks. Light industrial and warehouse units leave pallet racking, workbenches and whatever stock or materials didn't get sold or moved on. None of it is necessarily rubbish — some of it may have resale or scrap value — but all of it has to be out before the space can be shown to anyone else.

Business Waste Is Not the Same as Household Waste

This is the part landlords sometimes miss. A householder clearing their own home doesn't need a waste collection permit to move their own belongings. A commercial unit is different: anyone collecting and disposing of waste from a commercial premises on a paid or commercial basis needs to be an authorised waste collector holding a permit from the National Waste Collection Permit Office. If you're arranging the clearance yourself rather than through the tenant, make sure whoever you hire is permitted to collect and dispose of commercial waste — not just operating as a casual man-with-a-van. Getting the Unit Back to a Lettable State

Clearing the contents is usually only the first step. A unit that's been cleared but still has broken shelving fixed to the walls, cabling hanging from a suspended ceiling, or a layer of dust and grime from months of stock movement isn't going to show well to a prospective tenant or their agent. It's worth deciding up front how far the clearance needs to go: contents only, or contents plus stripping out fittings that the outgoing tenant installed and should have removed under the yield-up clause. Getting both done in one visit, rather than contents now and a strip-out later, is usually the more efficient route if you already know the unit needs both.

Timing It Against the New Tenant

If you already have an incoming tenant lined up, the clearance deadline is usually fixed by their own start date, and there's rarely much slack in that kind of handover. If you don't yet have a new tenant, there's more room to plan the clearance around getting the unit photographed and listed as soon as possible — an empty, clean unit shows and lets far better than one still full of a previous occupant's fittings. Either way, booking the clearance as soon as the outgoing tenant's departure date is confirmed, rather than waiting to see what they leave behind, keeps the gap between tenants as short as possible.
