The Real Factors That Drive House Clearance Cost

Every clearance company that prices properly is really working through the same handful of variables, whether they say so explicitly or not. Understanding them means you'll know roughly what shape your own quote is likely to take, and you'll be able to tell the difference between a company that's actually assessed your job and one that's pulled a number out of the air.

Volume of Contents

This is the single biggest driver of cost, and it's more specific than "how big is the house." Two three-bedroom houses can have wildly different volumes of actual stuff to clear — one lightly furnished and tidy, the other packed floor to ceiling in every room. Volume is usually thought of in terms of van loads, because that's the practical unit that determines vehicle size, crew size, and disposal cost downstream. A property that fills a quarter of a van is a different job to one that needs three full loads, even if the two houses are the same size on paper.

Property Size and Number of Rooms

Related to volume but not identical to it — a bigger property with more rooms generally means more time spent moving between spaces and often more furniture simply because there's more floor space to put it on. A one-bedroom apartment and a five-bedroom house are different jobs even before you know what's inside them, because the number of rooms affects how long the clearance takes independently of the total volume.

Access and Stairs

Access is one of the most underestimated cost factors by people getting a quote for the first time, and one of the most significant in practice. A ground-floor property with a driveway right outside the front door is a straightforward job — furniture goes from the room to the van with minimal effort. Compare that to a third-floor apartment with no lift, where every sofa, wardrobe, and box has to be carried down two or three flights of stairs by hand. The volume of contents might be identical in both cases, but the second job takes considerably longer and needs more physical effort from more people, and that time cost is real and has to be reflected in the price. Large furniture also often has to be manoeuvred around tight turns and landings, sometimes requiring doors to be taken off hinges — none of which is needed with clear, ground-floor access.

Distance From the Property to the Loading Point

This is separate from stairs, and it catches people out. If a van can park directly outside the front door, the carry distance for every item is a few metres. If the property is down a long rural lane, in an apartment complex where the van has to park in a basement or separate block, or on a street where the van has to sit further away because of restricted parking, every single item now involves a longer carry. Multiply that across a full house clearance and it adds up to real time — which is why access and loading distance are asked about at the quote stage rather than assumed.

Number of Staff and Vehicle Loads

Crew size is scoped to the job, not fixed. A small partial clearance might need two people; a full house with heavy furniture, several flights of stairs, and a tight deadline might need four or five, particularly for large or awkward items that genuinely need more than one person to lift safely. Vehicle loads follow the same logic — some clearances fit in a single van, while larger or higher-volume properties need two or three loads, meaning more time, more fuel, and more trips to a disposal or recycling facility. Photos of every room, not just the obviously cluttered ones, give a far more realistic sense of both than a rough verbal description.

Whether Sorting or Setting Items Aside Is Requested

Clearing a property indiscriminately — everything goes, nothing gets checked — is quicker than clearing it methodically while identifying documents, keepsakes, or specific items to keep, donate, or hand over to someone else. Both are legitimate ways to approach a clearance, but they're not the same job. Sorting takes longer because it means going through boxes, cupboards, and drawers with attention rather than moving furniture straight from room to van, which is why a job with detailed sorting instructions is priced differently to a straightforward clear-out.

Disposal and Recycling Requirements

Not everything that leaves a house goes to the same place. Furniture and household goods in good condition can go to donation or resale, general recyclable material gets separated out, and only genuine waste goes to a licensed transfer station. Doing this properly — rather than sending one mixed load straight to landfill — takes more handling than simply throwing everything into one skip, and disposal costs themselves vary depending on the material and the facility. Urgency and Timing

A clearance that can be scheduled flexibly, weeks in advance, is easier to slot in around other jobs than one that needs to happen tomorrow because of a sale deadline, a viewing, or a lease ending. Urgent or same-day jobs sometimes mean rearranging crew and vehicle availability at short notice, which is a real logistical cost even though the job itself might be a completely average size.

Full Clearance vs Partial Clearance vs Single-Item Removal

The pricing logic changes depending on which of these three you actually need, and it's worth being clear about which one applies to your situation before you ask for a quote.

A full house clearance covers the entire property — every room, and usually the attic, garage, shed, or garden if those are part of it too. Because the volume and scope are largest here, this is typically priced around total van loads, crew size, and how many areas of the property are involved, worked out from photos or a description of the whole job rather than room by room.

A partial clearance is narrower — a single room, a specific set of items, or "everything except what we've set aside in the spare room." This is a genuinely different job to a full clearance, not just a smaller version of the same price, because it often still needs the same access considerations (stairs, distance to the van) for a fraction of the volume. Being specific about what is and isn't included when you ask for a quote avoids confusion on the day about what was actually meant.

A single-item removal — one sofa, one mattress, a fridge that's died, a wardrobe that didn't survive a house move — is its own category again, closer to a rubbish removal or junk collection job than a full clearance. What Does House Clearance Actually Cost in Ireland? General Market Context

We don't publish a fixed price list for house clearance, for the same reason no honest clearance company does — the factors above genuinely change the cost from job to job, and a number that ignores all of them isn't a real quote. What we can offer instead is honest context on how the wider Irish market for rubbish removal and waste clearance tends to price this kind of work, based on published pricing from Irish providers rather than anything invented for this article. Treat the figures below as general market context, not a quote for work carried out by HouseClearances.ie.

Skip hire is the easiest comparison point because prices are more publicly standardised. Through 2026, mini skips (roughly 2–4 cubic yards) have commonly been priced somewhere in the region of €160–€250 in Ireland, with a 4-yard skip often landing around the €245 mark. A 6-yard skip — a common size for a house clearance's worth of waste — has generally run from around €280 up to €350 or more depending on where you are, with Dublin tending to sit toward the higher end because of demand and the council permit that's usually needed if the skip has to sit on a public road, and other Leinster counties often coming in a little lower. Those figures cover the skip itself, delivery, collection, and licensed disposal — not the labour of filling it, which is worth remembering when you compare a skip against a clearance service that includes the loading.

General rubbish and junk removal in Ireland is more commonly priced by volume than by a flat call-out fee — most operators work in fractions of a van (an eighth, a quarter, a half, a full load) rather than charging the same regardless of how much you actually have. Published Irish pricing guides for this kind of work typically span from well under €100 for a single small item up to several hundred euro for a full van load, with VAT, labour, transport, and licensed disposal included in the price rather than added on afterwards. A full house clearance sits at the larger end of this kind of volume-based thinking, and often needs more than a single van load once furniture, an attic, and a garage or shed are all factored in — which is exactly why the specific factors covered above matter more for a whole-house job than a general "per van load" figure ever could.

House Clearance Cost vs Skip Hire Cost

Skip hire and a clearance service solve the same underlying problem — getting rid of a large volume of stuff — but the real cost comparison is wider than the headline price of the skip itself. We've written more on this specific trade-off in our piece on why a clearance service is often a genuine alternative to hiring a skip, but it's worth summarising the parts that affect cost directly.

A skip's price doesn't include loading it. Every item — every sofa, every box, every bag — still has to be carried out of the house and lifted over the side by you, or by whoever you've arranged to help. For a full house clearance involving heavy furniture and multiple rooms, that's a genuinely significant amount of physical labour, and it's easy to underestimate both the time it takes and the risk of injury from awkward lifting. That labour has a real cost even when it doesn't show up on an invoice.

A skip on a public road usually needs a council permit, which takes time to arrange and adds to the overall cost on top of the hire fee. Skips are also priced by size, and it's a common mistake to underestimate how much a house clearance actually produces — meaning either a second skip gets ordered partway through, or items simply don't fit. And because everything that goes into a skip is treated as one mixed load, none of it gets sorted for reuse or recycling the way a proper clearance service handles it — which matters if keeping waste out of landfill is something you care about, not just the bill.

None of this means a skip is a bad option — for ongoing renovation or DIY work where you want somewhere to keep adding waste over several weeks, a skip genuinely makes sense. For a one-off house clearance, though, the full cost comparison includes your own time and labour, the permit, and the lack of sorting, not just the number on the skip company's price list.

Cost Differences for Specific Situations

The general cost factors above apply to every job, but a few specific situations change the shape of the pricing in their own way.

Attic, Garage and Shed-Inclusive Clearances

A house clearance that includes an attic, garage, or shed adds volume and, often, access difficulty on top of the main property. Attics in particular are more physical to clear than a normal room — working through a hatch, on a ladder, sometimes with low headroom and no proper flooring — which is why attic contents are usually assessed as their own part of the job rather than folded invisibly into "the house." Garages and sheds tend to hold heavier, bulkier items than an equivalent volume of household contents, which can affect how many staff and how much vehicle space the job needs. Mentioning these spaces upfront, with photos if possible, means they're factored into the quote from the start rather than discovered as an extra on the day.

Bereavement and Probate Clearances

Clearing a family home after someone has died is one of the more common reasons people search for house clearance costs, and it comes with its own considerations that aren't purely about volume and access. Families are usually given time to identify sentimental belongings before anything is cleared, and the pace of the job can be adapted around what's manageable rather than rushed through in one visit. That flexibility doesn't have to mean an inflated cost — it means the same underlying factors (volume, access, sorting requirements) still apply, just handled with the sensitivity the situation calls for. Landlord and End-of-Tenancy Turnarounds

Landlords and letting agents clearing a property between tenancies are usually working to a real deadline — a new tenant moving in, a sale date, or a re-letting target — which puts urgency into the pricing conversation in a way a flexible household clearance doesn't always have. The volume involved can also be unpredictable, particularly where a tenant has left furniture or belongings behind, which is worth flagging clearly when you ask for a quote. Storage Unit Clearances

Storage unit and container clearances are priced on a slightly different basis again — more around the size of the unit and how full it is than the number of rooms in a house, since a unit is effectively one dense space rather than several separate ones. Facility access hours, loading bay bookings, and whether the unit is on the ground floor or requires a lift all factor in too. How to Get an Accurate Quote

The single biggest thing you can do to get an accurate house clearance quote is give the company enough real information to price against — and the fastest way to do that is photos. A few photos of each room (not just the messiest ones) tell a clearance company far more than a verbal description like "it's a normal three-bed house," because volume, furniture size, and general clutter level are all things a photo shows instantly and a description can undersell or oversell. For larger or more complicated properties, an onsite assessment sometimes gives a more accurate figure than photos alone, particularly where access is unusual.

A genuine quote process will usually ask about a specific set of things: roughly how much needs to be cleared and from which rooms, whether an attic, garage, shed, or garden is included, what access looks like, whether any sorting or setting-aside is needed, and roughly when you need the job done. If a company gives you a number without asking any of that, it's worth being cautious — a price that hasn't accounted for volume or access is a guess dressed up as a quote, and guesses are exactly what lead to a different figure appearing on the day.

It's also worth asking whether a company is insured and holds a valid Waste Collection Permit before booking on price alone. An unusually low quote sometimes means disposal costs haven't been factored in properly, which either isn't sustainable for the company or — more concerning — means the waste isn't going through a licensed route at all, something that can leave the property owner exposed if it's ever traced back through illegal dumping. None of this is about disparaging any particular company; it's simply worth checking before you commit, the same way you'd check a tradesperson's credentials for any other job in your home.

What's Included vs What Might Cost More

As standard, a proper house clearance quote should include the loading of everything being cleared, transport away from the property, and responsible disposal or recycling at the end — that's the core of the job, not an optional extra billed separately. Sorting for donation, setting aside specific items on request, and clearing associated spaces like an attic, garage, or shed are usually part of the same conversation at the quote stage rather than hidden fees that appear afterwards.

The one genuinely common exception is hazardous materials — things like paint, chemicals, gas bottles, asbestos-containing material, or anything else requiring specialist handling rather than standard household waste disposal. These often need separate arrangements because they can't legally go out with a general clearance load, and disposing of them properly involves different routes and, usually, a different cost. If a property has anything like this, mentioning it at the quote stage means it's planned for as part of the job rather than discovered — and potentially causing delay — on the day itself.

How much does house clearance cost in Ireland?

Do clearance companies charge by the room or by volume?

Yes, significantly. A ground-floor property with a van parked outside is a quicker, less physically demanding job than an upper-floor apartment with no lift, or a property where the van has to park a long distance from the front door. Both factors add real time to the job.

A genuine quote should make clear whether VAT is included so there's no surprise later. It's a reasonable question to ask any company you're getting a quote from.
