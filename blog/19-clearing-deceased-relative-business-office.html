Clearing a Deceased Relative's Business or Office

When someone dies while they're still running a business — a shop, a small office, a workshop, a trade unit — the family or executor is often left with a premises to clear on top of everything else that follows a death. It's a different job to clearing a house. There's usually a lease or licence to think about, stock or equipment that may have value, business records that can't simply be thrown out, and a landlord or unit owner waiting to hear a date. Here's what tends to come up.

Why It's Different From a House Clearance

A home clearance is mostly about sentimental sorting and getting the property ready for sale or handover. A business premises adds layers: stock that might be sellable rather than simply disposed of, equipment that could be specialist or valuable, and paperwork that isn't just personal correspondence but client records, invoices, and financial documents with their own retention rules. There's also usually a harder deadline — many commercial leases and licences carry ongoing rent or service charges, so every week the unit sits full is a week that costs the estate money.

The Legal Side Comes First

Before anything is physically cleared, it's worth being clear on the legal position, and this is genuinely a job for a solicitor or accountant rather than a clearance company. If the deceased was a sole trader, the business itself doesn't continue — its assets and any debts become part of the estate, to be dealt with by the executor or administrator once a Grant of Representation is issued. If a registered business name was used, the Companies Registration Office needs to be told within three months of the business ceasing to trade. If the business was a limited company, the position is more involved again — a company needs a director, and one dying without a replacement being appointed can eventually put the company at risk of being struck off the register, so this is worth raising with a solicitor early rather than after the unit has already been cleared.

Financial and business records generally need to be retained for six years after a business closes, so before anything goes in a skip or a van, it's worth checking with whoever is handling the accounts what needs to be kept, and where. Confidential Documents and Data Protection

Most business premises hold some mix of client files, staff records, invoices, or financial paperwork containing other people's personal data, and that doesn't stop being covered by data protection law just because the business has closed. Documents that are being kept should go somewhere secure rather than into a general clearance load, and anything genuinely due for disposal should go through confidential shredding rather than a standard recycling or waste stream. If you're not sure which is which, it's safer to set a box aside and have someone check it than to guess.

Stock and Equipment: What Might Have Value

Unlike a typical house clearance, a business unit can contain things worth selling on rather than donating or recycling — stock-in-trade, tools, catering or salon equipment, shop fittings, computers, or specialist machinery. It's worth having someone who understood the business, or a trade contact, take a look before a clearance date is set, since value here can be easy to miss if the person clearing the unit doesn't recognise what they're looking at. Anything with resale value is worth separating out before the rest is cleared as general waste and recycling.

What the Day Itself Looks Like

Once the legal position is settled and anything of value or anything to be retained has been separated out, the physical clearance itself runs much like our standard office clearance work — furniture, fittings, general stock and waste sorted and removed, with donation and recycling used ahead of disposal wherever items are in reusable condition. Access matters more with commercial units than houses: loading bay availability, lift access in a shared building, and whether a landlord or management company needs advance notice all affect how the day is planned, so it's worth flagging those details when you get in touch.
