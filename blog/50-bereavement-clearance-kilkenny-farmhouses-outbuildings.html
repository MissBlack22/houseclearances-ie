Bereavement Clearance in Kilkenny: Farmhouses, Outbuildings and a Lifetime of Tools

When a parent dies in a rural part of County Kilkenny, the family is left with more than a house. There is the house, and then everything around it: a garage, a hayshed or barn, smaller sheds, a yard, and a lean-to nobody has opened in years. A lifetime on your own ground accumulates tools, machinery, timber, feed, fuel and spare parts in a way a suburban semi never does, and the sons and daughters dealing with it may live in Kilkenny City, in Dublin or abroad.

This guide covers planning that clearance building by building, what we take and what goes another way, where the papers tend to be kept, how the family decides about tools, and what a truck needs to get down a lane. It is general practical guidance, not legal advice; the solicitor handling the estate confirms what may be sold or removed, and when. Why a Farmhouse Is a Different Clearance

The house is usually the smaller part of the job; its kitchen, parlour and bedrooms hold roughly what any family home holds. The difference is outside. A single hayshed can hold more than the house, and what it holds is heavier, dirtier and more mixed: machinery parts, fencing wire, gates, timber, old doors, drums, feed bags, a workshop's worth of tools, and furniture moved out decades ago that never got further than the yard. So the quote has to cover every building, the day has to be planned around the weather and the ground, and the family has to decide about things that never come up in a town house: machinery, fuel, chemicals and tools a neighbour might want.

Walk the Whole Place First

Before anyone sorts anything, spend one visit walking every building with a phone and a notebook. Open every door, including the ones that stick. Photograph each building from the doorway and then in sections, and write a line for each: what it is full of, whether the roof is sound, whether anything is heavy, and whether the floor is safe. A rotten loft floor or a loose roof sheet is a reason to stay out until someone has looked at it. Include the yard, the garden, any trailer used as storage, and the oil tank. That list is the plan and the basis of the quote.

Where the Papers Hide in a Farmhouse

The documents an executor needs are rarely in one place. Alongside the kitchen dresser, look in the hot press, behind the clock on the mantelpiece, in the parlour sideboard, in a tin or shoebox in a wardrobe, and in the places a farmer kept things to hand: a shelf in the shed that served as an office, the tractor cab, the glovebox of the car, a bag hung on a nail in the feed shed. Herd and scheme paperwork, land maps, deeds, insurance papers and letters from the solicitor or the bank turn up in all of these.

Gather every paper into one box before the clearance and take it to the solicitor; our guide to old paperwork and documents during a clearance explains what to keep. Papers, photographs and personal items that turn up during the clearance itself are set aside and shown to you rather than removed, in the sheds as much as in the house.

Tools and Equipment: Deciding With the Family

Tools are where farm clearances slow down. They carry more memory than furniture, and several people may feel they should have them. Photograph them in groups, share the photographs with everyone who has a say, and set a kind but definite date for answers. A neighbour or relative who still farms may be glad of working machinery, gates and fencing material, which is often the best reuse there is. Anything unclaimed by the date goes with the clearance.

What comes with us

Hand tools, power tools, workbenches, ladders, wheelbarrows, mowers and strimmers once the fuel is drained, timber, old doors, fencing wire, gates, ordinary metal roofing sheets, empty clean drums, bicycles, garden furniture, old appliances, and the general overflow of a yard. Scrap metal and good tools are sorted for reuse and recycling rather than treated as waste.

What needs a dealer, an auction or a scrap merchant

Tractors, trailers, quads, cars and larger agricultural machinery are not part of a house clearance. They usually go through a dealer or an auction, to a neighbour, or to a scrap merchant, and the solicitor should know about them first. Send photographs anyway and we will say what we can and cannot take.

What We Cannot Take From a Farm

As an authorised waste carrier there are things we are not permitted to carry, and a farm has more of them than a town house.

  • The oil tank and the oil in it. Ask your heating-oil supplier or a tank specialist.
  • Gas cylinders. Back to the supplier.
  • Fuel. Old petrol, diesel and oil in cans, drums and machinery. Drain anything we are taking.
  • Agricultural sprays, chemicals and weedkiller. Ask the council about the correct route.
  • Veterinary medicines and sharps. Ask the vet or the pharmacy.
  • Paint, solvents, batteries. A civic amenity site or a hazardous waste collection; see household versus hazardous waste.
  • Anything that may contain asbestos, such as some older corrugated roofing or cladding. We cannot assess or take it; do not break or move it, and ask a qualified professional.

The Feed Shed and the Things That Are Not Household Waste

A shed that held animal feed needs its own line in the plan. Old feed attracts rodents, so expect droppings and nests and tell us if you see them; we do not offer pest control, and an active infestation may need a professional first. Feed bags, meal bins and the clutter of the shed come with us once the feed has been dealt with. Silage wrap, bale netting and other farm plastics are generally not treated as household waste; the council can tell you the recycling route, and the solicitor may need to account separately for anything that belongs to the farming business.

Getting a Truck Down the Lane

Our box truck is wider and a good deal taller than a car. Tell us how wide the lane is at its narrowest, whether branches hang low, how wide the gateways are, whether the yard is soft after rain, and whether there is room to turn. If the truck cannot reach the yard the job is still possible, but the carrying distance changes how long it takes and belongs in the quote rather than being discovered on the morning. A short video driving in from the road answers most of it. Tell us too whether animals are still on the land, who will open gates and hold keys, and whether the electricity is on in the sheds.

The Order of Work Over Two or More Days

  1. The family's visit. Papers to the solicitor, photographs and personal items gathered, keepsakes into one room, tools photographed and offered round.
  2. Hazardous items separated. Fuel, chemicals, cylinders and paint put together where we can see and leave them.
  3. The house. Room by room, floors and doorways protected, reusable furniture kept apart from waste.
  4. The garage and workshop. Tools loaded last, so a late change of mind can still be honoured.
  5. The sheds and the yard. The heaviest, wettest and dirtiest part, planned for a dry day where possible.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you clear the sheds, barn and yard as well as the farmhouse in Kilkenny?

Yes. We quote the house and every outbuilding together from your photographs and plan the work over as many days as needed.

Do you take tractors, trailers or farm machinery?

No. Vehicles and larger agricultural machinery are not part of a house clearance and usually go through a dealer, an auction, a neighbour or a scrap merchant. Hand tools, power tools, workbenches and drained mowers do come with us.

What happens to the oil tank, the gas cylinders and old fuel?

We cannot take them. The heating-oil supplier or a tank specialist deals with the tank, cylinders go back to the supplier, and for old fuel, sprays and chemicals, ask the council about the correct route.

Can a neighbour take tools and equipment before you clear?

Yes, and it is often the best outcome for working machinery, gates and fencing material. Agree it with the family and the solicitor, set a collection date before the clearance, and tell us what has gone.

Will the truck get down a narrow farm lane?

Usually, but tell us the width of the lane and gateways, any low branches and the state of the yard. If the truck has to stop short, the carrying distance is planned into the quote.

Where are papers usually found in a farmhouse?

The dresser, the hot press, the parlour sideboard, a tin in a wardrobe, a shelf in the shed that served as an office, the tractor cab and the glovebox of the car. Anything personal found on the day is set aside and shown to you.

Does the clearance have to wait for probate?

Ask the solicitor handling the estate. Securing the buildings, separating hazardous items and gathering papers can generally happen straight away; removing or selling contents usually waits until the solicitor and the beneficiaries agree.
