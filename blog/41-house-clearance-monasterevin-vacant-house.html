House Clearance in Monasterevin: Clearing a House That Has Been Empty for Months or Years

Most house clearance advice assumes a home that is lived in, or was lived in until very recently. Some of the properties we are asked about are different. A house has stood empty since a relative moved away, or died, or since a tenancy ended and nobody quite got round to dealing with what was left. The owner may live in another county, or abroad, and has not been through the door in a long time. They have a key, a vague idea of what is inside, and a feeling that it will not be pleasant.

Why Empty Houses Are a Different Job

A house that people live in is, whatever its faults, being looked after. Windows are opened, heating comes on, small leaks are noticed, post is picked up. A house that stands empty loses all of that, and a number of small problems can build up unnoticed.

  • Damp and mould develop where heating and ventilation stop.
  • Small leaks become larger ones, particularly from roofs, gutters and old pipes.
  • Pests move in. Mice and rats are the most common; birds and insects may nest in attics and chimneys.
  • Stored items deteriorate. Paper, textiles, mattresses and upholstered furniture hold moisture and can become unpleasant.
  • Utilities may be off, or unsafe. No light, no running water, and electrics that have not been used for years.
  • Security weakens. Locks, windows and outbuildings may not be as secure as they were, and a house that looks neglected can attract attention.
  • The owner's memory of the contents is out of date. What you think is there and what is there are often different.

None of this means a vacant house cannot be cleared. It does mean that the first visit should be a careful one, and that the quote should be based on what is actually there, not on what you remember.

Before You Go In: A Safety Check

Do not go in alone if you can avoid it, particularly if the house has been empty for a long time. Tell someone where you are going. Wear sturdy shoes and old clothes, and bring a torch, gloves and a mask. Then walk the outside before you go in.

Outside

  • Look at the roof, chimneys and gutters for obvious damage or sagging.
  • Check for loose or broken tiles, slates or panels.
  • Look at doors, windows and outbuildings for signs of forced entry or neglect.
  • Note any overgrown paths, brambles, ivy or fallen branches that could block access.
  • Look for signs of animals, nests or droppings.
  • Check any oil tanks, gas bottles or sheds for leaks or damage.

Inside

  • Open the door and let the house air for a few minutes before you step in.
  • Smell is a useful warning. Strong damp, rot, or the smell of gas or oil means you should stop and take advice.
  • If you smell gas, do not turn on lights or electrical switches. Leave and contact the gas emergency service.
  • Look at floors, ceilings and stairs for soft patches or sagging before you put weight on them.
  • Check for water stains on ceilings and walls.
  • Do not touch electrics that look damaged or damp.
  • Be careful with the attic hatch and loft ladder, which may be stiff or unsafe.

If the house is in poor structural condition, a clearance may not be the first step. A builder or surveyor may need to look at it first. Tell us what you see and we will say whether we can proceed, or whether something else should happen first.

What You May Find Inside

The contents of a long-empty house are usually a mix of what was there when it was left and what has happened since. Expect some of the following.

  • Furniture and household goods exactly as they were left, sometimes decades out of date.
  • Food and drink in presses and the fridge, often long past its date.
  • Post piled behind the door, or in a box if someone has been collecting it.
  • Papers, photographs and valuables that nobody has looked at in years.
  • Damp-affected items: mattresses, sofas, books and clothing.
  • Signs of pests: droppings, nests, chewed material.
  • Items left by others: a former tenant's belongings, or things that have been stored by relatives.
  • Hazardous materials that we cannot take: old paint, solvents, oils, chemicals, fuel, gas bottles, batteries, and in older properties some building materials.

Damp, Mould and Pests

Damp and mould

Damp and mould are common in empty houses, and the severity varies. A little surface mould on a wall or a damp mattress is one thing; widespread mould or damp-affected structure is another. If you see significant mould, wear a mask, avoid disturbing it more than you need to, and tell us in advance. Items affected by damp are not usually reusable, and are disposed of rather than donated. Longer term, the cause of the damp matters more than the clean-up: a leak that is not fixed will bring the problem back.

Mice, rats and other pests

Droppings, nests and gnawed material are a sign of rodents. They are a health concern, particularly because droppings can carry disease and disturb dust. Wear gloves and a mask, do not sweep dry droppings, and do not touch nests or dead animals with bare hands. If you think there is an active infestation, tell us so that we can plan, and consider calling a pest control professional first. We do not offer pest control. We can clear the contents once the situation is safe.

Birds, wasps and insects

Attics, chimneys and outbuildings can house nests. A wasp nest in an attic is a serious hazard for anyone going up. Do not disturb it. Ask a professional to deal with it before the attic is cleared.

Items you should not try to salvage

Mattresses, upholstered furniture, carpets and soft toys that have been damp or exposed to pests are best removed, not cleaned. The same applies to food, open cosmetics and old medicines.

Asbestos and Other Building Materials

Some older buildings contain materials that can be hazardous if broken or disturbed, including certain roofing sheets, ceiling tiles, pipe lagging and insulation. If you suspect a material contains asbestos, do not break, drill, sand or move it. Ask a qualified professional for advice before anything is done. Suspected asbestos cannot be treated as ordinary waste, and it is not something we can assess. Tell us if you have any concerns when you send photographs.

Utilities and Services

Light and water make a clearance safer and quicker, but in a long-vacant house they may be off or not working properly. Before you arrange a clearance, find out:

  • whether the electricity is connected, and whether the fuse board is safe to use
  • whether the water is on, and whether pipes have leaked or frozen
  • whether there is a gas supply and whether it has been isolated
  • whether there is an oil tank, and whether it has any oil in it
  • whether the alarm, if any, is still active, and who has the code

If something is unsafe, do not try to restore it yourself. Contact the supplier or a registered tradesperson. If there is no light, tell us so that we can plan to bring our own.

Insurance and Responsibility

An empty house is a different risk to an occupied one, and insurers know it. Policies often include conditions for properties that have been unoccupied for a certain period, and a claim can be affected if the conditions are not met. We cannot tell you what your policy says. Do three things:

  1. Read the policy wording, or ask the insurer directly, about unoccupied properties.
  2. Tell the insurer about the situation honestly, and ask what it requires.
  3. Keep a written record of the answer.

If the property belongs to an estate, tell the solicitor, since the executor has duties in relation to the property. Our guide to clearing a house before probate sets out the questions to ask, and the executor's checklist covers the paperwork.

The First Visit: What to Do and What to Leave

The aim of the first visit is to find out what is there, not to clear it. Take your time and do not feel you have to see everything in one go.

  1. Take a notebook and phone. Photograph everything, room by room, with the doors and presses open.
  2. Look for papers and valuables first. Check the places people keep important things: drawers, tins, handbags, coat pockets, the top of wardrobes. Take documents, photographs and valuables with you. Be cautious with anything damp or mouldy.
  3. Check the attic and outbuildings, if it is safe, and photograph what is there.
  4. Note the access: the lane, the door, the stairs, the distance to where a van can stand, any step or gate.
  5. Note any hazards, including leaks, pests, gas, oil and old chemicals.
  6. Do not start sorting. That is for another day, once you know what you are dealing with.
  7. Lock up and secure the property before you leave.

After the Clearance

An empty, cleared house shows its true condition. This is often the right moment to decide what happens next.

  • Take dated photographs of every room.
  • Get the building assessed if you saw signs of damp, leaks or structural problems.
  • Arrange cleaning, repairs or decoration if you plan to sell or let.
  • Read the meters and tell the utility providers.
  • Review the insurance, now that the house is empty and clean.
  • Consider the garden. An overgrown garden may need attention too; see garden waste clearance and the guide to garden clearance before selling.

Arranging It From a Distance

Many vacant houses belong to people who live elsewhere. If that is you, there are some ways to make a long-distance clearance workable without travelling more than you need to.

  • Use a local helper for the first look. A relative, neighbour or friend can walk the property with a phone and send photographs and video while you are on a call.
  • Agree a key-holder. One person who opens the house, meets the crew and locks up.
  • Make a list of what you want. Even if it is only a few items, write it down so the helper knows what to look for.
  • Ask for photographs at the end. A final walk-through by video is often enough.
  • Have the paperwork ready. Keep the title documents, insurance papers and the solicitor's details in one place.
  • Be honest with the helper about what to expect. A long-empty house is not pleasant, and nobody should be surprised alone.

Gardens, Yards and Outbuildings That Have Been Left

The outside of a vacant house tends to deteriorate faster than the inside. Brambles, ivy, fallen branches and long grass can block paths, hide steps and make a van's access difficult. Outbuildings may be rotten, full of stored material, or home to nests. If access is blocked, tell us in advance, and consider having the worst of the growth cut back before the clearance. We clear green waste and sheds alongside the house, and one quote can cover them. Take care with anything unstable: a collapsing shed roof or a rotten floor is a risk, and nobody should go in until it has been looked at.

What Not to Throw Out in a Vacant House

It is tempting to treat a neglected house as rubbish. Resist that until you have looked carefully, because the places people store important things are often the most overlooked.

  • Envelopes of cash in a book, a tin or a coat pocket.
  • Savings books, share certificates, insurance policies and pension papers.
  • Title documents and deeds, often in a drawer or an old biscuit tin.
  • Photographs, letters and cards in bags, boxes and suitcases.
  • Jewellery, watches and medals in odd places.
  • Small valuables or collections: coins, stamps, records, antiques.
  • Keys that may belong to outbuildings, safes, vehicles or other properties.

Go through the house carefully, wearing gloves, before any clearance. Damp papers should be dried and checked, not discarded. If you find a large quantity of papers, sort them into keep, check and discard, and ask a solicitor or accountant about anything you are not sure of. We do not offer shredding or data destruction, so keep sensitive papers aside and deal with them yourselves.

Questions Owners of Vacant Houses Ask Us

"Should I clean first?"

No. Clearing first and cleaning afterwards is far more efficient. Cleaning a house that is full of contents means cleaning around things you are about to remove.

"Should I fix the damp first?"

Find the cause, because a leak that continues will keep damaging the building. But you do not need to repair it before clearing, and in many cases the clearance shows how bad it really is. A builder or surveyor can advise.

"Is it worth keeping the furniture?"

Sometimes. Solid wood furniture often survives better than upholstered pieces, and can be cleaned and reused. Anything that has been damp or visited by pests is best removed. A quick test is smell and touch: if it is musty, damp or sticky, it goes.

"How do I know if the electrics are safe?"

You do not, without a qualified electrician. Do not use sockets or switches that look damaged or damp. If you plan to sell, let or renovate, an electrician's inspection is worth arranging.

"What if I find something I did not expect?"

Stop, do not move it, and take advice. For items of potential legal or safety concern, contact the Gardaí. For items of value, contact the solicitor if the estate is involved.

Common Mistakes With Vacant Houses

  • Going in alone and unprepared. Take someone with you and tell another person where you are.
  • Assuming it is as you remember. It is not.
  • Disturbing droppings or suspected asbestos. Take advice first.
  • Leaving valuables and papers in a damp house. Remove them early.
  • Not telling the insurer. It can matter later.
  • Hiding the real condition from the clearance company. It makes the quote wrong and the day harder.
  • Using an unpermitted operator. A lower quote is not worth waste dumped with your name attached.
