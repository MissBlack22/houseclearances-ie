Bereavement Clearance in Greystones: A Timeline From the Funeral to the Sale

When a parent dies in Greystones, the people left to deal with the house are often not in Greystones. Sons and daughters live in Dublin, in another county or abroad, and the house on the North Wicklow coast becomes a place they travel to for a day at a time, on the DART or down the N11, with a list and not enough hours. The work itself is rarely the problem. The problem is knowing what to do when, so that nothing is thrown away, sold or signed for before it should be.

This guide sets out a timeline, from the first fortnight after the funeral to the day the keys change hands. The months are indicative: every estate moves at its own pace, and the solicitor, the executor and the sale set the dates, not the clearance. It is general practical guidance, not legal advice; the solicitor handling the estate is the right person to confirm what may be sold or removed, and when. Why a Timeline Helps When the Family Is Not Nearby

Clearing a house from a distance goes wrong in two predictable ways. Either everything is left until the week before the sale closes and dealt with in a panic, or someone starts bagging clothes in the first week and a sibling who could not travel feels decisions were made without them. A timeline fixes both. It gives each visit a purpose, keeps the family in step with the solicitor, and leaves the clearance itself to a single agreed day near the end. Greystones is reachable by DART for a family member without a car, and each trip should end with something done.

The First Fortnight: Secure the House and Do Very Little Else

In the first two weeks the house needs minding, not clearing.

  • Lock up and list the keys. Find out how many sets exist, who holds them, and whether the garage, shed or alarm have separate keys or codes. If a neighbour already has a key, ask them to keep it; they will be useful later.
  • Tell the insurer. Home insurance often has conditions about unoccupied houses. Ask what they are, and ask about the heating over winter.
  • Deal with the perishable. Empty the fridge, take out the rubbish, and collect or redirect the post.
  • Photograph every room as it is. Not for a quote yet, but as a record for the whole family of what was in the house before anything moved.

Resist clearing anything in this period, even the obvious rubbish in the shed. The executor has a duty to look after the estate's assets, and what can be removed before probate is covered in our guide on whether you can clear a house before probate is granted. The short version is that securing and tidying are fine, and anything of value waits.

The First Month: Papers, Valuables and the Visit That Matters Most

The first proper visit is the one to plan for. Its purpose is to find and set aside everything that must not be lost.

  • Find the will and the papers. Deeds, bank and pension documents, insurance policies, passports. Put them in one box and take them with you or to the solicitor. Our guide to old paperwork and documents during a clearance covers what to keep.
  • Anything that might be valuable. Check pockets, handbags, drawers, biscuit tins and the backs of wardrobes. Make a simple list.
  • Photographs, letters and albums. Gather them into one place. They are the items most often regretted.
  • Keepsakes. Ask every sibling, before the visit, for a short list of the things they would like, and walk the house with those lists. Where two people want the same thing, photograph it and decide later.

Choose one room as the "keep" area and put everything set aside in it. Nothing else needs to be sorted on this visit. Our guide to clearing a loved one's home after a death has more on sharing keepsakes without falling out.

The Second to Fourth Month: Waiting for the Solicitor, Using the Time Well

For many families there is now a gap of several months while the solicitor gathers information and applies for the grant of probate. The timing varies from estate to estate, and the solicitor is the only reliable source for yours. The gap is not wasted time. It is when the sorting happens and the clearance is arranged, so that the day itself can go ahead as soon as the solicitor says so.

Sort in rounds, one visit at a time

Work through the house in rounds rather than room by room: first the things people want, then the things that can be sold or donated, then everything else. Each round can be a separate Saturday. Label furniture that is staying for a relative, and keep the "keep" room closed. Do not start paying for a storage unit for things nobody has decided to keep; our guide to when a storage unit costs more than it is worth explains why. If the house holds far more than a usual family home, our guide to hoarder clearance describes the slower, more careful approach that suits it.

Check the empty house on every visit

Walk through each time you arrive and look for damp patches, dripping taps, a boiler fault light and signs of mice. A neighbour who glances at the house between visits is worth a great deal; ask them to ring you if anything looks wrong.

Agree the sequence with the estate agent

If the house is to be sold, speak to the agent before fixing a date. Some agents prefer to photograph a house lightly furnished; others want it empty. Ask what they would like left in place and tell us the same, so the clearance leaves exactly what the photographs need.

When the Solicitor Gives the Go-Ahead: The Clearance Itself

Once the solicitor confirms the contents can go, the clearance is a single agreed day, or two for a larger house, between the family's last keepsake visit and the agent's photographs.

The week before

  • Make the final pass of the "keep" room and take everything in it away with you.
  • Label anything staying for the agent, a relative or the buyer.
  • Empty and defrost the fridge and freezer if they are going.
  • Separate paint, solvents, oils, fuel, chemicals, batteries and gas cylinders. We cannot take these; see household versus hazardous waste for where they go.
  • Tell the neighbour the date, and confirm who will hand over the key.

On the day, if a family member can attend

We work Monday to Saturday, 8am to 5pm, and never on Sundays, so a Saturday is possible for someone travelling down from Dublin. The day begins with a short walk-through to confirm what stays and what goes. After that you can stay, or leave and come back for the final walk-through. The crew clears room by room, protects floors and doorways on the route out, and keeps reusable items separate from waste.

If nobody from the family can attend

Many clearances happen with nobody from the family present, and it works well when the groundwork above has been done. The crew collects the key from the neighbour or the agent, works from your labels and the video walk-through, and sends photographs of each room when the house is empty. Anything personal that turns up unexpectedly, such as papers or photographs, is set aside and shown to you rather than removed. Keys go back to whoever you nominate. Before the Agent's Photographs: Empty, Swept and Presentable

The clearance should finish a few days before the agent's photographer is due, not the morning of. That leaves time for a cleaner, a gardener, or a plumber for the dripping tap you noticed in month two. We leave the rooms clear and swept of loose debris. If the garden has grown wild while the house was empty, our guide to garden clearance before selling explains what is worth doing, and shed and garage clearance can be included in the same visit. Take your own photographs of the empty house as well, as a record for the executor's file.

Closing: The Last Visit

The final trip to Greystones is short. Check the attic, the shed and the garden one last time; something is nearly always left in the attic. Take meter readings and photograph them. Gather every set of keys, including the neighbour's, and hand them to the solicitor or the agent as agreed. Close or transfer the utilities and insurance on the date the solicitor gives you, and take the last box of "not sure" items home with you.

The Timeline at a Glance

  1. First fortnight: secure, insure, empty the fridge, photograph every room. Clear nothing.
  2. First month: papers, valuables, photographs and the family's shortlists set aside.
  3. Until probate is sorted: sort in rounds, check the house each visit, quote from photographs, agree the sequence with the agent.
  4. Solicitor's go-ahead: final keepsake pass, labels, hazardous items separated, clearance day.
  5. Before the photographs: a few days' margin for cleaning and repairs.
  6. Closing: last check, meter readings, keys handed over, utilities and insurance closed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How soon after the funeral should we book a bereavement clearance in Greystones?

There is no set time, and nothing about the clearance itself sets the pace. Most families wait for the solicitor's go-ahead on the contents, which can be several months after the death. You can send photographs for a quote at any stage and fix the date when the solicitor confirms.

Can the clearance be done on a Saturday so the family can travel down?

Yes. We work Monday to Saturday, 8am to 5pm, and not on Sundays. Tell us early if a Saturday is the only day that suits.

What happens if nobody from the family can be at the house on the day?

The crew collects the key from a neighbour, the agent or the solicitor, works from your labels and the walk-through done by video, sets aside anything personal that turns up, sends photographs of the empty rooms, and returns the keys to whoever you nominate.

Can we leave some furniture in place for the estate agent's photographs?

Yes. Tell us which pieces are staying and label them. We clear around them. If they need to go later, send a photograph and we quote that as a separate visit.

Do we need the grant of probate before the house can be cleared?

This is a question for the solicitor handling the estate. Securing the house and sorting papers are generally fine straight away; removing or selling anything of value is usually left until the solicitor and the beneficiaries agree. Our guide on clearing a house before probate explains the general position.

Can the shed, garage and garden be cleared in the same visit as the house?

Yes, and it is usually simpler to do them together. Include them in your photographs. Paint, solvents, oils, fuel, chemicals, batteries and gas cylinders cannot come with us and should be separated beforehand.

What should we do with the things we cannot decide about?

Put them in one box or one room, labelled, and take them home with you or leave them with a relative. It is far easier to clear something later than to retrieve it after a clearance.
