Hoarder Clearance in Dublin, When It Is a Parent's Home

Most families do not see it happen. A parent who always kept things keeps a little more each year, the spare room fills, then the hall, then the kitchen table. By the time anyone says the word hoarding, the house has changed, and the person living in it has usually stopped having visitors.

Our page on house clearance in Dublin explains our service in general, our hoarder clearance page covers how we work in any home that has filled up, and our guide to clearing a hoarded home in Dublin covers the process step by step. This page is for sons and daughters. It is about the three situations we are asked about most, and about the part of the job that families find hardest: deciding.

Three Situations, Three Different Jobs

The house may look the same in each. The job is not.

Your Parent Still Lives There

This is the most delicate of the three, because it is their home and they are in it. Nothing leaves without their agreement. Usually the first visit is a partial clearance: the hall, the stairs, a path through the kitchen and the bathroom, so the house is safe to move around in and someone can get to the boiler and the windows.

We work at your parent's pace, in stages if that is easier, with a pause between visits so nobody feels the house is being taken away from them. Some homes genuinely need to be worked through in stages, and agreeing that early avoids an argument on the day.

Your Parent Is Moving Into Care, or In With Family

Now there is usually a date, and a smaller room to fill. The first job is choosing what goes with them: the chair they always sit in, photographs, a few pieces of furniture, the things they reach for every day. Those are packed and set aside before anything else happens.

The rest of the house can then be cleared in one go or in stages, depending on whether it is being sold, let or kept. If your parent wants to be involved in what happens to their things, we can work around a visit from them.

Your Parent Has Died

A large number of hoarder clearances happen after a death, when a family is left with a house that built up over many years. Grief and the size of the job arrive at the same time, often with brothers and sisters living in different places.

Here the decisions belong to the family and the executor. Papers come first, because hoarded homes often hold important documents among everything else. Then the family chooses, and then the clearance. Our page on bereavement clearance covers the executor's side.

The Family Decides, Not the Crew

The hardest part of clearing a parent's hoarded home is rarely the carrying. It is the deciding, and the disagreements that come with it. A few things help.

  • One person speaks for the family. We take instructions from one named person. The others can be asked, but on the day the crew needs one answer to each question.
  • Agree the keep list before the day. Photographs, papers, a few pieces of furniture, anything a brother or sister has asked for. Everything on it is set aside, and nothing set aside is loaded.
  • Sentimental things are never assumed to be rubbish. However they are surrounded. If the crew is not sure about something, it is put aside and shown to you.
  • Papers are kept apart. Bank letters, certificates, deeds and unopened post often turn up in the most unlikely places. They go into a box of their own.
  • If your parent is living there, they have the last word. It is their home.

What Can Be Saved

A hoarded home is rarely all rubbish. Things were often bought and never used: bedding in its packet, kitchenware in its box, clothes with the tags on. Good furniture is often hidden behind years of boxes. All of it is sorted by hand, room by room, not by the bag, so that what can be kept is kept and what can be used again is not thrown away.

Some things cannot be saved, and it is kinder to say so. Anything damp, mouldy, soiled or damaged by pests is disposed of properly. If you have seen signs of pests, or there is a hygiene issue in the house, tell us when you send photos, so we can plan the day around it.

Part of Our Job Is to Donate

For many parents, and many families, it is easier to let things go when they know they will be used. Part of our job is to donate. When we clear a home, furniture and household things that are clean and still have use in them are set aside and passed on, to individuals who need them and to charity organisations. If there is something in particular your parent would like donated rather than cleared, tell us and we set it aside separately.

We do not name the charities we work with, and nothing on the family's keep list is ever given away. Whether a particular piece can be donated depends on its condition and on whether someone can use it, so we do not promise it for any one item. What cannot be donated is recycled where possible, and the rest goes to an authorised waste facility under our permit, NWCPO-25-13287-01.

What We Take, and What We Cannot

We take

  • Furniture, beds and mattresses
  • Bags, boxes and stored household items
  • Newspapers, magazines and paper
  • Clothes, bedding and kitchenware
  • Appliances and electricals
  • The attic, the garage and the shed

We cannot take

  • Paint, varnish and wood stain
  • Solvents, oils and fuel
  • Chemicals
  • Batteries
  • Gas cylinders, full or empty
  • Asbestos or clinical waste

We stand the things we cannot take together and tell you where they are, so they can go to a civic amenity site or a hazardous waste collection, and a gas cylinder back to a supplier.

Frequently Asked Questions

My mother still lives in the house. Can you clear it without upsetting her?

We can work at her pace, in stages, and nothing leaves without her agreement. Often the first visit is a partial clearance of the hall, the stairs, a path through the kitchen and the bathroom, so the house is safe to move around in. More can follow when she is ready.

Can it be done in stages?

Yes. Some homes are best worked through in stages, with pauses between visits. We agree that as part of planning the job, and the quote covers every stage.

Who decides what is kept?

If your parent lives there, they do. Otherwise the family or the executor. We take instructions from one named person, and anything the crew is not sure about is put aside and shown to you.

Will the neighbours know?

What happens in the property, and who arranged the clearance, stays between us. Tell us if the day needs to be especially discreet and we plan around it.

Can things from the house be donated?

Yes, where they can be used. Furniture and household things that are clean and still have use in them are set aside and passed on, to individuals who need them and to charity organisations. If there is something your parent would like donated, tell us and we set it aside separately.

Do you name the charities?

No. We do not name the charities and organisations we pass things to. If you are a charity that needs furniture or household items, call us on 083 090 4545.

What about papers and documents?

Hoarded homes often hold important papers among everything else. Bank letters, certificates, deeds and unopened post are kept apart in a box of their own and handed to you.

There may be pests in the house. Can you still do it?

Tell us when you send photos. Pest and hygiene issues change how the day is planned, and in some homes pest control has to come first. We will tell you honestly what we can and cannot do.

Do we need to be there?

Not necessarily. Some families prefer to be there throughout; others arrange access and are elsewhere while the work happens. We send photos when each stage is done.
