House Clearance in Kilcullen: Emptying a House Before a Renovation or Extension

Not every house clearance is about leaving. A good number are about staying: a family in Kilcullen has bought an older house and wants to renovate it, or has lived in the same house for twenty years and is finally replacing the kitchen, adding an extension, converting the attic or redoing the floors. The builder has a start date, and before they can begin, the house has to be emptied, or at least the part that is about to be worked on.

That raises a set of questions that are different from a sale or a bereavement. What needs to go and what can be stored? Who removes the old kitchen? What happens to the furniture while the work is done? How do you keep dust and damage away from what is staying? And how do you fit a clearance around a builder whose schedule you do not control?

This guide is for homeowners in Kilcullen and the area around it who are planning building work. It is practical, and it is about the contents of the house and how to move them out of the way. It is not building advice: your builder, architect or engineer is the right person for that. For the general service, see our House Clearance Kilcullen page.

Why Clear Before the Builder Arrives?

You can start building work in a full house, and many people do. But there are good reasons to clear first.

  • Safety. Furniture and boxes in the way of tradespeople are trip hazards and fire risks.
  • Protection. Dust, water and paint get everywhere. Belongings left in work areas can be damaged.
  • Speed and cost. Builders who have to move your things around work more slowly, and some will charge for it.
  • Access. Floors, walls and ceilings can only be worked on if they can be reached.
  • Insurance and liability. Contents damaged or lost during work can be a source of dispute. Ask your insurer and your builder what is covered.
  • Decision-making. A renovation is a good moment to decide what you actually want in the house. Keeping everything because there is no time to sort it is how clutter gets carried into the finished room.

Three Kinds of Belongings in a Renovation

Everything in the affected part of the house falls into one of three groups. Deciding which is the first job.

1. Things you are keeping and will need during the work

Clothes, kitchen basics, a bed, the TV, children's things, work equipment. These stay in the house, away from the work area, or move with you if you are living elsewhere during the build.

2. Things you are keeping but will not need until the work is finished

Seasonal items, books, spare furniture, ornaments, rarely used appliances. These need somewhere to go for a while: another room, the garage, a relative's house or a storage unit. Our guide to when a storage unit costs more than it is worth helps you decide whether storage is worth it. We do not offer a storage or moving service.

3. Things you are not keeping

Furniture you will not replace, old appliances, broken items, boxes that have not been opened since the last move. These are for a clearance. Doing it before the work starts means you finish with a cleaner, emptier house, not just a newer one.

The ratio of the three groups is usually a surprise. People who think they will need storage often find that most of what they were going to store can go.

Fitted Units, Appliances and Fixed Items

Renovations involve things that are attached to the house, and it is worth being clear about who deals with them.

  • Fitted kitchens. Where it is agreed in advance, we can remove old kitchen units and appliances as part of a clearance. But disconnecting and capping gas, water and electrics is work for a qualified tradesperson, and should be done before the units are taken out.
  • Appliances. Cookers, dishwashers, washing machines and fridges can be removed once disconnected. Retailers who sell you a new appliance are generally required to take back the old one, so ask at the point of sale before you arrange its removal.
  • Wardrobes and fitted furniture. Built-in wardrobes and shelving may be part of the structure. Agree with your builder who removes them, and tell us whether they are included in the clearance.
  • Flooring, doors and sanitary ware. Strip-out work is usually part of the builder's scope. If it is, the builder will normally arrange the waste. If you want us to remove loose items such as old doors and carpets, say so. Do not assume.
  • Heating oil tanks, boilers and radiators. These are plumbing and heating jobs. Ask your builder or installer who is dealing with them.

The rule is to be explicit. Tell us what is included, ideally with photographs, and agree in advance who is responsible for each item. Disputes in renovations usually come from assumptions.

Working Alongside Your Builder

A clearance should fit around the building schedule and not the other way round. Some questions to settle with your builder before you book anyone:

  1. When do they need the house, or the area, empty? A clearance can only be planned once you know the date the builder is working to.
  2. What are they taking out themselves? Old kitchens, floors, walls and fittings are often part of the builder's work. Do not pay twice for the same job.
  3. What are they not taking? Furniture, personal belongings and general contents are rarely included.
  4. Where can waste skips go? A builder's skip may block the drive or the road. Plan access for a clearance van as well.
  5. Can areas be sealed off? Dust sheets and temporary doors protect the rest of the house.
  6. Who has the keys? If you are not living there, agree who opens up and locks up.
  7. What happens if the schedule slips? Builders' dates change. Tell us early and give us a window rather than a single day, and expect that we cannot promise a date until we have seen the job.

If the work is structural or involves the roof or chimney, ask your builder whether any room should be emptied for safety, not just for convenience.

Room by Room: What to Clear Before Different Kinds of Work

Kitchen replacement

Empty all presses, drawers and worktops. Pack kitchen essentials in a box for temporary use. Take crockery, glasses, small appliances and food out of the room. Clear a path for the units. Decide what to do with the old units, cooker and fridge, as above.

Extension

The area next to the extension is often disturbed by building work, knocked through, or opened up. Empty the adjoining rooms of furniture and fragile items. Clear the garden of anything that will be in the way: sheds, play equipment, garden furniture, anything on the line of the footprint. We clear sheds and garden waste.

Attic conversion

The attic has to be empty before conversion. In most houses it holds decades of boxes and stored items. Our attic clearance service handles the hatch and ladder, and the guide to what is worth keeping from an attic helps with the decisions.

Garage conversion

Garages are among the fullest spaces in a house. Our garage clearance service and the guide to where to start with a garage cover it.

Flooring, plastering and decorating

Every room that is being worked on needs to be completely empty. Furniture is moved out and stored, or cleared. Rooms next door benefit from protection too.

Bathrooms

Remove toiletries and personal items. The sanitary ware is typically the builder's or plumber's job.

Rewiring and heating

Floors may be lifted and walls opened throughout the house. In practice that means clearing most of the house, or at least moving everything to the centre of each room under sheets.

Buying an Older House: A Special Case

Some of the renovations we hear about start with a house that has just been bought, and that comes with contents. A house in Kilcullen may have been sold with furniture, boxes, garage contents or the previous owner's belongings left behind. Before you start building, check:

  • what was agreed in the sale about contents, and what the solicitor says
  • whether anything of value or sentiment should be returned to the seller or their family
  • whether the attic, garage, sheds and outbuildings have been fully inspected
  • whether there are hazards such as old fuel, chemicals, gas bottles and, in older buildings, materials that might contain asbestos

Do not break, drill or move material you suspect contains asbestos. Ask a qualified professional first. A house that has been empty for a long time may also have damp, pests and neglected utilities; our guide to clearing a house that has been empty for months or years explains what to check.

Hazardous and Special Items

Renovation projects turn up items that need separate handling.

  • Paint, solvents, adhesives and chemicals. We cannot take these. Do not pour them away; take them to a civic amenity site or a hazardous waste collection. See household versus hazardous waste.
  • Gas bottles and fuel. We cannot take them. Return gas bottles to the supplier.
  • Batteries and electrical items. Recycled through electrical waste routes.
  • Plasterboard, rubble and building waste. This is part of the building process and is normally dealt with by the builder. Clearance is for household contents.
  • Asbestos. Not an item for a house clearance. Take professional advice.

A Sensible Order of Events

  1. Agree the scope of the building work and the date window with your builder.
  2. Decide what you are keeping, storing and clearing, room by room.
  3. Remove papers, valuables and keepsakes from the house, or to a safe room.
  4. Arrange storage or somewhere to put what is staying.
  5. Get a quote for the clearance by sending photographs.
  6. Agree who removes fitted items, appliances and old units.
  7. Clear the house, or the affected areas, with enough margin before the builder's start date.
  8. Protect what remains, with dust sheets and sealed doors.
  9. After the work, bring back only what you have decided to keep.

If You Are Living in the House During the Works

Many families do not move out. They live in one part of the house while another is being worked on. That makes the clearance more of a reorganisation than an emptying, and it is worth planning.

  • Choose a "safe room". A bedroom or living room that stays clear of dust and work, with a bed, a few clothes and the essentials.
  • Set up a temporary kitchen. A kettle, microwave, a small cooler or fridge, a few plates and cutlery, in a room that is not being worked on. Kitchen renovations can last a long time, and a basic set-up makes it bearable.
  • Seal off the work area. Ask your builder about dust barriers and temporary doors.
  • Keep valuables and papers in the safe room or out of the house.
  • Protect floors on the route. Tradespeople will walk through rooms that are not being worked on.
  • Keep pets and children away from the work. Dust, tools and open floors are hazards.
  • Plan for the weekend. Tradespeople often do not work at weekends, which is the time you most notice what you have given up.

Replace, Reuse or Remove? Deciding What Comes Back

The end of a renovation is the best moment to be strict about what returns. If you have cleared the house, you have a rare opportunity to bring back only what you want. A few questions help.

  • Have I used this in the last year?
  • Does it fit the new room, in size and style?
  • Would I buy it again today?
  • Is it in good enough condition to be worth keeping?
  • Am I keeping it for myself, or because I feel I should?

Furniture that is sound but does not fit the new design can be sold or donated. Furniture that is worn out is better cleared than kept in a spare room. For help with the decisions, see our guide to what to keep, sell, donate or clear and what to do with an old sofa you cannot sell.

Reusing Fittings and Fixtures

Some renovation waste is not waste. Doors, radiators, fireplaces, sinks, light fittings and solid timber can sometimes be reused or sold, particularly in older houses. If you think something might have value, ask your builder before it is removed. Salvage dealers and reclamation yards may be interested, and you can keep items to reuse yourself. If you do not want them, tell your builder or us as early as you can, because once something is in a skip it is gone.

Budget Traps to Avoid

  • Paying for storage you do not need. Sort first, then store only what you are keeping.
  • Paying a builder's day rate to move furniture. A clearance may cost less than a tradesperson's time spent shifting boxes.
  • Not getting the scope in writing. A builder's quote should say whether it includes strip-out and removal of old fittings.
  • Discovering hidden contents late. A full attic or garage that is only found on the day delays everything.
  • Leaving the clearance to the last minute. A rushed clearance rarely gets sorted well.
  • Treating a clearance and a skip as the same thing. A skip is a container you load yourself. Our guide to skip hire versus a clearance service explains the difference.

Common Mistakes

  • Booking the clearance before knowing the builder's dates. Settle the window first.
  • Paying twice for strip-out. Agree who removes fitted items.
  • Assuming the builder will take furniture. Usually not.
  • Storing everything. Storage costs add up. Sort first.
  • Leaving valuables in the house. Take papers, jewellery and keepsakes out of the work area.
  • Not asking the insurer. Contents damaged during works are a common dispute.
  • Not mentioning hazards. We cannot take paint, fuel, chemicals or suspected asbestos.
  • Not leaving enough margin. Dates move. Leave a buffer.
