Bereavement Clearance in Athy: Arranging It When You Live Elsewhere

Many of the families who contact us after a death in Athy are not in Athy. A parent lived in the family house for decades, and the children now live in Dublin, in another county or abroad. They have jobs and families, a limited number of days off, and a house that needs dealing with. They cannot be on site every day, and they do not want to lose control over what happens to the contents.

This guide is for that situation. It covers how to organise a bereavement clearance in Athy from a distance, calmly and without having to be there for the whole of it. Start With One Person and One Plan

Remote clearances go wrong when siblings each speak to the company separately. Before anyone calls, agree three things:

  • One point of contact. One person speaks to the company and passes on decisions. If there is an executor, it is usually them.
  • One list of requests. Everyone sends their wishes to that person in writing: what they would like, what must not be thrown out, and when they can visit.
  • One cut-off date. A date after which the house is cleared whether or not everyone has been. It sounds blunt, but without one the job drifts for months.

If the estate has a solicitor, tell them when you plan to clear and check that nothing needs to stay in the house for valuation or legal reasons. Our guide to clearing a house before probate sets out the questions to ask.

Keys and Access

The practical question for a remote job is how the crew gets in. Typical arrangements are:

  • a relative or neighbour who lives nearby meets the crew and hands over the key at the start
  • a key is left with a trusted person and returned afterwards
  • a family member travels for one morning only, to open the house, do a short walk-through and hand over

Check how many keys exist, whether the garage, shed or side gate have separate ones, and whether there is an alarm to turn off. Share an alarm code only by phone or message with the person who needs it, and change it afterwards.

Getting a Quote Without Being There

You do not need to visit before asking for a quote. Ask someone nearby, or make one short visit, to photograph and film:

  • every room, with wardrobe and press doors open
  • the attic, including the hatch and how you get up
  • the garage, shed and any other outbuildings
  • the front of the house, the driveway or road, and any steps or gates
  • any large or heavy items, such as a piano, safe, freezer or a wardrobe that does not come apart

Send them by WhatsApp, with the address area, the type of property and your target date. For most jobs this is enough for a quote without a visit. If the job is large or the access unclear, we will tell you honestly whether a visit would help.

Older Houses and Outbuildings

Family homes that have been lived in for a long time often have more than a garage and a shed: a store room, a workshop, a lean-to, an old coal house. They are easy to forget when sending photos, and they are often where the heavy and awkward items are. Walk the whole site and include every building. We clear garages, sheds and garden waste alongside the house, so everything can be in one quote.

A long lane, a gate or a narrow road can affect where the van stands and how far things are carried. Mention anything like that at the quote stage. Keeping Control of What You Care About

The fear most people have about arranging a clearance from a distance is that something precious will go by mistake. A few habits remove most of that risk.

  1. Do a video walk-through with someone in the house and you on the phone, before the clearance date. Point at what you want.
  2. Put what you are keeping in one room and close the door, or label it clearly. Tell the crew that room is not to be touched.
  3. Move papers and valuables out beforehand. Documents, photographs, jewellery, cash, medication and keys should leave the house first. Check coat pockets, the top of wardrobes, tins, handbags and the back of drawers.
  4. Tell the crew what you hope to find. If there was a particular watch, set of letters or box of photographs, say so. Anything that looks personal will be set aside for the family.
  5. Agree what happens to the rest. Reusable items are separated for donation or resale where realistic, and everything else is recycled or disposed of through licensed routes.

Signing Off the Job Remotely

You do not need to be present at the end to know the job is done. Ask for:

  • a final walk-through by the person holding the key, covering each room, the attic and the outbuildings
  • photographs or a short video of the empty property
  • confirmation that the key has gone back to the person you named
  • a final cost that matches the quote

Dated photographs of the empty house are also useful for the estate, for an agent if it is going on the market, and for a landlord if it was rented.

What Happens on the Day

The crew arrives, confirms the plan with the key-holder, and protects floors and hallways where needed. They work room by room, starting with large furniture, and clear the attic and outbuildings as agreed. Reusable items are separated from waste, which leaves through a permitted route. Most single-property clearances take one visit, and a large house with several outbuildings may take longer; we say so when we quote. Before Any Clearance: Looking After the Empty House

When someone dies, their home does not stop needing attention. Whoever is dealing with it usually discovers a short list of small, practical jobs that nobody mentioned. None of them are urgent in the first hours, but they matter in the first weeks, and they are especially easy to miss when you live a distance away.

  • Keys. Find out how many sets exist and who has them. A neighbour, a carer or a cleaner may hold one. Write the list down.
  • Insurance. Contact the home insurer and ask whether anything changes now that nobody lives in the house. Policies often include conditions for unoccupied homes, and it is better to ask than to find out after a problem. We cannot tell you what your policy says, so read the wording or ring them.
  • Heating and water. In winter, do not let the house freeze. In any season, check for dripping taps and anything that could leak.
  • Food and bins. Empty the fridge, freezer and food presses and put out the bins before they become a problem. It is the least pleasant job, and one of the easiest to put off.
  • Post. Arrange for post to be redirected or collected so that bills, statements and official letters do not pile up. You will need to see them.
  • Appearances. A house that looks empty can attract attention. Some families ask a neighbour to keep an eye on it, put a light on a timer, or call in regularly.

Banks, utilities, pension providers and other organisations also need to be told. The full list is in our executor's checklist, which is worth working through before the house is emptied, because many of the papers you need are in the house.

Sorting: Do It in Rounds

It is tempting to try to go through the whole house in a weekend. Most families find that this does not work, because the sorting is slow, emotional and full of small discoveries. A calmer approach is to work in rounds, and to leave room between them.

Round one: the things that must not be lost

Papers and valuables come first: the will, bank and pension papers, the deeds, insurance documents, passports, birth, marriage and death certificates, cash, jewellery, watches and keys. Gather them in one place, ideally a box you take with you. If you hand any of it to someone else, photograph it first and note who has it.

Round two: the things people want

Let each family member walk through the house, alone or in pairs, and mark what they would like with sticky notes or on a shared list. Photographs, jewellery, clocks, a particular chair, books, a coat. It is easier to agree on a list than to bargain in the room. When more than one person wants the same thing, taking turns to choose usually settles it better than a debate. Remote family members can do this by video call, with someone in the house pointing the camera at each item.

Round three: the things that can be sold or donated

Furniture, china, books and clothing in good condition can often go to a charity shop or be sold. Charity shops set their own rules, so ring first. Our post on what happens to your belongings after a clearance explains what we do with items that are left for us.

Round four: everything else

This is the part a clearance company takes over: the clothes nobody wants, worn furniture, broken appliances, the contents of the garage and shed, boxes in the attic. By now most decisions have been made, and what is left is volume and effort.

What to Do With Particular Kinds of Belongings

Photographs, letters and personal papers

These matter most and are the easiest to lose in a bag of papers. Keep them together, even if you do not have the heart to go through them yet. A box in your own house to look at later is a perfectly good decision. Photographs can be scanned or photographed on a phone, so that every sibling has a copy.

Medication

Unused or out-of-date medication should be returned to a pharmacy, not put in the bin or left for the clearance crew. Do this before the clearance date.

Phones, tablets and computers

These may contain photographs, messages and banking details. Keep them aside until the family has decided what to do, and take advice before wiping or disposing of them. Do not include them in a clearance unless you are sure they hold nothing you need.

Clothing

Clean clothing in good condition is welcome at most charity shops. Many families keep one or two items, a coat, a scarf or a jumper, because of what they mean. There is no wrong choice.

Jewellery, watches and anything valuable

Check pockets, handbags, drawers, tins and the backs of presses. If you suspect that something is valuable, such as an antique, a collection or jewellery, get advice or a valuation before it leaves the house, and tell the solicitor if the estate is involved.

Food, medicines and hazardous items

Perishable food should go first. Paint, fuel, chemicals and gas bottles are handled separately from general waste; see household versus hazardous waste. If you find anything you are unsure about, such as items you would not expect in a house, ask for guidance before the day and contact the Gardaí if appropriate.

Furniture and appliances

Good furniture can be offered to family, sold or donated. Appliances that no longer work are recycled as electrical waste. Check that gas appliances are disconnected safely before anything is moved.

Sharing Keepsakes Without Falling Out

The clearing of a family home is where long-standing family tension tends to surface. A few habits reduce the risk.

  • Agree the method first. Take turns, draw lots, or use a shared list, but decide before anyone starts choosing.
  • Separate sentimental from financial. A modest item can be priceless to one person and worthless to another; it is also not the same as something with real resale value, which belongs to the estate.
  • Write down what is agreed. A short email or message is enough, and avoids disagreements about what was said later.
  • Let people say no. Nobody should be forced to take something. Anyone who does not want an item is entitled to say so.
  • Do not decide on behalf of someone who is not there. If a sibling cannot attend, send photographs and give them time to answer.

A Timeline, Without Pretending to Know Your Family

There is no right speed. Some families clear within weeks because a sale or a tenancy has to move. Others wait months, or do it in two parts. This is a rough order of events, to adjust to your own circumstances.

  1. The first days: secure the house, list the keys, tell the insurer, deal with perishable food.
  2. The first weeks: locate the will and key papers, speak to the solicitor if there is one, start the notifications in the executor's checklist.
  3. When the family is ready: a first visit, round one and two of sorting, and a decision on who will deal with the clearance.
  4. Quote and date: send photographs, agree a date that suits the key-holder and any sale or tenancy timeline.
  5. Before the clearance: remove papers, valuables and keepsakes, label what is staying, empty the fridge.
  6. Clearance day: walk-through, clearance, final walk-through.
  7. Afterwards: photographs of the empty house, return of keys, and the next step, such as cleaning, a valuation, an estate agent or a tenant.

If a sale is planned afterwards, tell us the date. If you are not ready for everything, a staged clearance is fine. Our guide to clearing a deceased relative's business or office may also be relevant if the person ran a business from home or from premises.

If the House Is Going on the Market, or Being Rented

Many families clear a house because it is about to be sold, rented or handed back to a landlord. In each case, tell the company the plan, so the result is what you need.

  • Sale. Agree with the agent or solicitor what stays with the property, such as fitted wardrobes, kitchen appliances or garden fittings, and share the list. Clear the house before photographs where you can.
  • Rental. A landlord may need the house empty before a re-let. The practical points are in our guide to preparing a rental property for new tenants.
  • Handback to a landlord. If the person rented the house, check the lease and notice period with the landlord, and keep dated photographs of the empty property.

Worries We Hear Most Often

"What if I make the wrong decision?"

Almost every family worries about this. The best protection is to do the irreversible steps last: papers and valuables first, family choices second, donation third and disposal last. If you are unsure about an item, set it aside in a box you take with you. Most decisions are less final than they feel.

"What if the house is in a state?"

Some homes have a lot in them, or have not been looked after in the final months. We do not judge a home by how it looks, and the process is practical and discreet. Tell us honestly what we should expect so that we can plan the right number of people and equipment.

"What if there are things nobody wants?"

That is normal, and it is what the final round is for. Reusable items are separated for donation where realistic, and the rest is recycled or disposed of responsibly.

"What if a sibling disagrees?"

Do not clear anything that is in dispute. Agree the process first, write it down, and if necessary ask the solicitor for advice. A clearance can happen in stages, leaving disputed items until they are settled.

"Can we keep the house as it is for a while?"

Yes. There is no obligation to clear on any particular date, unless a sale, a tenancy or the estate requires it. The practical points about insurance, heating and security still apply.

"Will someone from the family have to be there?"

No. A key-holder at the start and a final walk-through, by video or photographs if need be, is enough for many families.

A Few Words on Pace and Kindness

A house clearance after a death is a practical job, and it is also a goodbye. Some people want to be there for every room. Some cannot face it. Some want to look at one thing only and leave. All of these are normal. We take our lead from the family, we do not rush people, and we do not judge what a home looks like. If you tell us what matters, whether it is a particular room, an item or a date, we plan around it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you arrange a bereavement clearance in Athy if I live abroad?

Yes. Photos or a video, a named key-holder and a final walk-through are usually all that is needed. Everything can be agreed by phone, WhatsApp or email.

Who needs to be at the house on the day?

Someone who can open the house and confirm what stays and goes, at least at the start. After that, the crew can work and you or the key-holder can check the result at the end.

What if the family cannot agree who gets what?

Settle that before the clearance. Agree a list, and keep disputed items out of the clearance until the family has decided or taken advice. The executor, with a solicitor if needed, has the final say on estate matters.

Can you clear sheds and outbuildings as well?

Yes. Include every building in the photos and video, and the quote will cover them with the house.

Can the clearance be done in stages?

Yes. Some families clear bulky furniture and outbuildings first and leave the personal rooms for a later visit.
