House Clearance in Newbridge: Clearing a Family Home Before a Sale or a Move

Newbridge is one of the busier commuter towns in County Kildare, and a lot of the houses we are asked about are family homes in the larger estates that grew up around the town. These are three- and four-bedroom houses that have been lived in for twenty or thirty years, with a garage, a floored attic and a shed that have collected the overflow of a family. When the house is about to be sold, or the owners are downsizing or moving on, all of that has to go somewhere.

This guide is for homeowners in that position. It is about timing and sequencing, because that is where most of the stress sits: how to fit a house clearance around a sale, what to do first, and what to leave until last. Why the Clearance Usually Comes Before the Estate Agent

It is common to wait until a house is listed before thinking about clearing it. In practice it works better the other way round. A clear house photographs better, shows better at viewings, and lets buyers picture their own furniture in it. Many estate agents will ask for the house to be tidied or emptied before the photographs are taken.

That does not mean emptying everything at once. A sensible order for most sales looks like this:

  1. Decide what is coming with you. Anything that is going to the next home, or to family, is separated first.
  2. Clear the spaces buyers open. Garage, attic, shed and spare room are the ones buyers look into, and the ones most likely to be crammed.
  3. Clear the bulky furniture you do not need. Extra beds, wardrobes, sofas and tables you are not taking.
  4. Keep the staging pieces until the photographs are taken, if the agent recommends it.
  5. Final clearance of everything left, before the keys are handed over.

You can do this as one job or in stages. We quote for the property as a whole, or for part of it, so you can start with the garage and attic now and leave the rest for completion week.

The Garage, Attic and Shed Problem

In a Newbridge estate house, the garage is often the first place to look. It started life as parking, became a store, and may now hold bikes, tools, paint, boxes from three house moves, and furniture that no longer fits anywhere. The attic is similar, often with a boarded floor and a hatch that is not easy to use. Both are slow to clear by hand, and neither is pleasant.

Things to know before you begin:

  • Paint, chemicals, gas bottles and batteries are handled separately from general waste. If you can sort them out first, do. Our guide to household versus hazardous waste explains the difference.

  • Garages often hide the heaviest items: workbenches, freezers, lawnmowers, shelving. Mention them when you send photos. See garage clearance and where to start with a garage.
  • Sheds are usually quick, but check for gas bottles, fuel and old chemicals. See shed clearance.

Sorting Before the Day: A Short, Realistic Plan

You do not need to pack. You do need to make decisions. A plan that works for most households is to give yourself two or three evenings.

  1. Evening one: papers and valuables. Documents, passports, jewellery, family photographs, keys, medication. Put them in a box that leaves the house.
  2. Evening two: what you are taking. Mark or move it to one room, and label it clearly so the crew does not touch it.
  3. Evening three: what could be sold or donated. Furniture and household goods in good condition. Ring a charity shop first, as they set their own rules on what they can take. If you cannot be bothered, ask us to separate reusable items.

What to Do With Different Kinds of Belongings

"Clear the house" covers dozens of different decisions. It helps to think in categories, because each one has a different best route. This is the list we find ourselves going through with homeowners, room by room.

Furniture

Solid wood furniture, good sofas and dining sets can often be sold or donated. Anything damaged, stained, wobbly or missing its fire-safety label is unlikely to be accepted by a charity shop, and some shops no longer take upholstered furniture at all, so ring first. Large items like wardrobes and beds are often easier to move if they are dismantled, which the crew will do on the day. If you are selling pieces yourself, photograph them early and set a firm collection date, because a sale that drags past the clearance date is a common cause of last-minute stress.

Mattresses and bedding

Mattresses are bulky and are not usually reusable, so they go as part of the clearance. Bedding, towels and curtains in good condition can go to a charity shop or an animal charity that takes textiles; worn items are handled as textile waste.

Electrical items and appliances

Fridges, freezers, washing machines, cookers, televisions and small electricals are classed as waste electrical and electronic equipment (WEEE) and have their own recycling route. If you are buying a new appliance for the next home, remember that retailers selling you a new one are generally required to take back the old one, so it is worth asking at the point of sale. For everything else, we take electricals to the appropriate recycling routes as part of the job. Empty and defrost fridges and freezers beforehand, and unplug them a day or two in advance if you can.

Clothing, shoes and textiles

Clean clothing in good condition is welcome at most charity shops. Bag it separately from the rest so it is not mixed with anything damp or dirty. Worn or damaged textiles can go to textile recycling rather than general waste, and the crew will separate them if you ask.

Books, DVDs and paperwork

Books can often be donated, and some charity shops are glad of them. Old paperwork is different: bank statements, medical letters, bills and anything carrying names, addresses or account numbers should be shredded or disposed of securely, not left in a bag by the door. Go through filing cabinets and drawers before the day and keep anything you may need, such as property documents, guarantees, warranties and tax records. If you are not sure how long to keep a document, ask your solicitor or accountant.

Kitchenware and household goods

Crockery, cutlery, pots, glasses and ornaments are among the easiest things to donate, and among the most time-consuming to sort. A box labelled for charity, packed before the clearance, saves a surprising amount of time. Chipped or odd items can go in the clearance.

Garden and outdoor items

Garden furniture, tools, bikes, children's toys and play equipment can be donated if they are in working order. Lawnmowers and strimmers should have their fuel emptied. Green waste can be cleared with the house; see garden waste clearance.

Paint, chemicals, fuel and gas bottles

These are the items most likely to cause delay. Do not pour them away and do not leave them in the shed for the crew to discover. Gas cylinders should go back to the supplier where possible. Tell us what you have when you send photos so that we can plan for it. Our post on household and hazardous waste explains how each type is treated.

Medication and personal care items

Out-of-date or unused medication should be returned to a pharmacy rather than put in the bin. Remove it from the house before the clearance.

Phones, laptops and tablets

Before any device leaves the house, remove personal data. Sign out of accounts, reset the device if you can, and take out SIM and memory cards. If you cannot, keep the device aside rather than including it in the clearance.

A Realistic Timeline, Working Back From the Handover

We do not promise a fixed duration for any clearance, because it depends on volume, access and crew size. But the planning stages around it follow a pattern, and working backwards from your completion or move date makes the whole thing manageable. These stages are a guide rather than a rule.

  1. As soon as you have a rough date: send photos for a quote and ask what lead time we need. Tell us if the date is fixed or flexible.
  2. Several weeks before: begin the three-evening sorting plan above. Sell or give away larger items that need a buyer or a collection.
  3. A fortnight before: book charity collections, if any, and confirm the date with the clearance company. Start the paperwork for any changes of address.
  4. The week before: finish moving anything you are taking. Empty the fridge and freezer. Pack papers and valuables and take them out of the house. Confirm access and parking.
  5. A day or two before: do a last walk-through with a bag for anything missed. Mark rooms that are not to be touched.
  6. Clearance day: walk-through with the crew, clearance, final walk-through.
  7. After the clearance: clean, make any small repairs, take dated photographs, and hand over the keys.

If your timeline is much shorter than this, say so. Many jobs are arranged at short notice, and we will tell you what we can and cannot manage.

The Clearance Day, Step by Step

It helps to know the order of events, so there are no surprises.

  1. Arrival and walk-through. We meet you or the key-holder at the house. We confirm which rooms are in scope, what is staying, what has been set aside, and any problem areas such as stairs or a tight driveway.
  2. Protecting the property. Where it is needed, floors, banisters and door frames are protected, and a clear route is set up to the van.
  3. Furniture and bulky items first. The rooms open up and everything else becomes easier to reach. Items that cannot pass through a door are dismantled.
  4. Contents, room by room. Smaller items, boxes and general contents are sorted into what is reusable, what is recyclable and what is waste.
  5. Garage, attic and shed. These are usually done once the house is clear, or in parallel if there are enough people.
  6. A sweep. Loose debris is swept up from the cleared rooms.
  7. Final walk-through. We go through every room and outbuilding with you or the key-holder, and agree that the job is done.

If something turns up that looks personal or valuable, such as photographs, letters, cash or jewellery, it is set aside, and we will ask you what you want done with it.

Common Mistakes Homeowners Make

  • Waiting until the week before completion. Even a short job needs a day, and some need more. Leaving it to the last minute removes any room for error.
  • Not telling the company about the attic or garage. These are often the largest parts of the job, and surprises make quotes inaccurate.
  • Leaving valuables in the house. Documents and jewellery should be out of the house before anything else happens.
  • Assuming everything is waste. A lot of what is in a family home can be donated or sold. Ten minutes on this saves money and avoids waste.
  • Forgetting what stays with a sale. If fitted wardrobes, appliances or garden items are included in the sale, tell the crew in writing.
  • Not checking the waste permit. A cheaper quote from someone without a permit can leave you responsible if the waste is dumped.
  • Booking a skip and doing it yourself, without counting the effort. Our post on skip hire versus a clearance service sets out the trade-offs.

Should You Keep the Furniture for Viewings?

It is a fair question. Furniture can help buyers imagine living in a house, and an entirely empty house can look smaller than it is. Agents have different views, and it depends on the house and the market, so ask yours. A common approach is to clear everything that is surplus now, keep the best pieces in the main rooms until the photographs and early viewings are done, and arrange a second, final clearance before completion. Doing it in two visits costs a little more planning, but it often suits a sale better than one visit at either end. Tell us if that is what you want, and we can quote for both stages together.

After the Clearance: Getting the House Ready

An empty house is a blank page. Before handing it over, most owners do some or all of the following:

  • a clean, from top to bottom, including the oven, windows and any marks left by furniture
  • small repairs, such as filling nail holes, touching up paint and fixing loose fittings
  • meter readings and final bills, with providers told about the change of occupancy
  • dated photographs of each room, for your own records and in case of a dispute
  • a check that all keys, remotes, alarm codes and manuals are ready for the new owner or tenant

We leave rooms swept and empty, and we will tell you if we think the property would benefit from a further clean before it is shown.
