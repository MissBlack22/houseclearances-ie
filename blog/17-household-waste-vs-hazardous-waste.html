What Counts as Household Waste vs Hazardous Waste in a Clearance

The bulk of what comes out of a typical property is ordinary household waste — furniture, bedding, clothes, boxes, general rubbish. Then there's the shelf in the garage. Half-used paint tins, a can of engine oil, a box of batteries, weedkiller, a gas bottle for a patio heater. That small pile is legally a different category of waste, and it's the reason a clearance can't always be a single load to a single destination.

The Line Isn't About How Dirty Something Is

People tend to assume "hazardous" means visibly grim. It doesn't. The classification is about what the material can do — burn, corrode, poison, or leach into water and soil — not how unpleasant it looks. A grease-caked oven is ordinary household waste. A sealed, tidy-looking half-litre of paint thinner is not. That's why sorting by appearance goes wrong so often.

What's Usually Hazardous in an Ordinary House

The list is more mundane than most people expect. Hazardous household items typically include aerosols, batteries, unused medicines, household and garden chemicals and cleaners, motoring products such as engine oil and oil filters, paints, varnishes, thinners and solvents, and general DIY products. They turn up in most houses, usually under the sink, in a shed, or on a garage shelf.

These are accepted at civic amenity sites that handle hazardous household waste, and many councils also run free hazardous waste collection days. One practical detail that catches people out: items generally have to be in their original, fully labelled packaging to be accepted. An unlabelled jam jar of some clear liquid nobody can identify is a problem, because nobody can safely say what it is. Unused medicines are usually best returned to a pharmacy rather than brought to a site at all.

What you must not do is pour paints or chemicals down a drain or sink, or put them in a household bin. Even small quantities go somewhere — into wastewater, groundwater or soil — and disposal points exist precisely so they don't.

Items That Need Their Own Route Entirely

A few things sit outside even the hazardous-waste-at-the-recycling-centre route.

Gas bottles and cylinders should never go in a bin or a skip. If they're crushed they can explode, and that risk lands on whoever is operating the machinery further down the line. They go back to a supplier or to a designated drop-off point.

Asbestos is the one to stop and take seriously, and it turns up in older properties as garage or shed roof sheeting, old insulation board, and some floor tiles. The advice in Ireland is to contact your local authority for guidance and only hand the material to a local authority collector or to someone who holds a permit to handle it. It is not a DIY job and it is not something to break up first. If you suspect it, leave it in place and get advice before anything else happens on site.

Electricals Are a Third Category

Anything with a plug or a battery is WEEE — waste electrical and electronic equipment. It isn't general waste and it isn't hazardous household waste; it has its own system. Household electrical items can be brought to civic amenity sites free of charge, and retailer take-back means a shop must accept an old appliance of a similar type free when you buy a replacement. In a full clearance, the fridge, the washing machine, the old TVs and the drawer of dead cables usually make up a meaningful chunk of the load.

Why Your Clearance Company Cares About the Difference

Every waste collector operating in Ireland needs a waste collection permit, and that permit specifies which regions they can collect in and which waste types they're authorised to carry. Hazardous waste is a separate authorisation, with its own paperwork trail for each movement. So a van permitted for household and commercial waste genuinely cannot take your paint tins — not as a matter of preference, but because doing so puts the permit itself at risk.

This is worth knowing when comparing quotes. Anyone offering to "take the lot, no questions" is either quietly leaving the hazardous items behind, or handling them in a way you'd rather not have your name attached to. Permits are publicly searchable, so a collector's authorisation is something you can check rather than take on trust. It's the same reason a proper clearance beats a skip for mixed loads — a skip can't legally take any of this either, as we covered in our guide to using a clearance instead of skip hire.

What To Do Before the Van Arrives

Pull the small stuff out first. Go through the under-sink cupboard, the shed and the garage shelves, and put anything in the categories above into one box, kept separate from everything else. Tell whoever is quoting what's in it — a photo of the shelf is enough. That way the hazardous items get routed properly instead of being discovered mid-load, and the rest of the clearance runs as one clean job.
