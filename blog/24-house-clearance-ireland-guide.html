What a House Clearance in Ireland Typically Involves

Regardless of which company does the work, a house clearance in Ireland tends to follow a similar shape. It usually starts with some form of assessment — photographs sent in advance, a phone description, or an onsite visit for larger or more complicated properties — followed by a quotation before anything is booked. From there, the actual work is fairly consistent: sorting through the property, removing furniture and general contents, loading it for transport, and disposing of everything responsibly at the end, whether that means recycling, donation, or a licensed waste facility for genuine rubbish.

There's an important distinction between a full clearance and a partial one. A full clearance empties an entire property — every room, plus an attic, garage, shed or garden where relevant — usually because the property is being sold, handed back, or nobody is keeping any of the contents. A partial clearance is more selective: one room, a single item like an old sofa, or everything except a list of things the owner is keeping. Most companies quote these differently, because the labour and time involved aren't the same even when the property is.

What gets cleared covers most of what accumulates in an ordinary home over the years — furniture, mattresses and bedding, boxes and paperwork, kitchen contents and appliances, clothing, books, and general household goods, along with garage, shed and garden contents where those spaces are part of the job. Certain items need separate handling rather than going out with the general load, which is where the regulatory side of Irish waste collection actually starts to matter.

Waste Regulation in Ireland: Why the Permit Matters

This is the part of house clearance that most people never think about until it directly affects them, but it's worth understanding because it protects the person hiring the clearance company, not just the company itself. In Ireland, anyone collecting waste commercially — including clearance companies, skip firms and "man with a van" operators — is legally required to hold a valid waste collection permit. These permits are issued and administered by the National Waste Collection Permit Office (NWCPO), a single national body (based at Offaly County Council) that was set up in 2012 to handle waste collection permit applications for every waste management region in the Republic of Ireland, replacing what used to be a patchwork of separate local authority permits.

A waste collection permit isn't a blanket licence to collect anything, anywhere. Each permit specifies the geographic regions a collector is authorised to work in, and the categories of waste they're authorised to carry — general household waste is one category, but hazardous waste (paints, chemicals, gas bottles and similar) requires its own separate authorisation. A company permitted to collect ordinary household waste genuinely cannot lawfully take a box of paint tins or a gas cylinder in the same load — not as a matter of preference, but because doing so falls outside what their permit allows. We've written about that split in more detail in our guide to household waste vs hazardous waste in a clearance, including which everyday items fall into which category and where they actually need to go.

Why this matters to you as a customer rather than just to the collector: Irish waste law places a duty of care on whoever hands waste over for disposal. If waste is handed to a collector who isn't properly authorised, responsibility for that waste doesn't simply disappear — it can come back to the person who handed it over, even where they didn't realise the collector wasn't permitted. Waste collection permits are a matter of public record, and the NWCPO publishes a searchable register so anyone can check whether a company is genuinely authorised, in which regions, and for which waste types, rather than taking a claim on trust. It's a reasonable thing to check before booking any clearance company, and a properly permitted operator won't mind being asked.

A couple of practical details are worth knowing if you're ever checking a collector's credentials yourself: a waste collection permit is granted for a five-year period rather than indefinitely, and permitted collectors are required to display their permit number on the collection vehicle and carry a copy of the permit with them. If a company can't or won't show either of those things on request, that's a reasonable reason to look elsewhere.

How a House Clearance Works, Step by Step

The exact detail varies by company, but the underlying process for a house clearance in Ireland is fairly consistent from job to job:

  1. Initial contact. By phone, email, or a quote form — with a rough description of the property and what needs clearing.
  2. Photos or an onsite assessment. For most properties, photographs are enough to work from. Larger or more complicated jobs are sometimes better assessed in person.
  3. A free quotation. Based on the actual volume and access involved, not a generic per-room estimate.
  4. Agreeing a date. Including any access details — parking, stairs, a lift to be booked, or a key arrangement if you won't be present.
  5. The clearance itself. The property is worked through methodically, with anything you've flagged as important set aside rather than cleared automatically.
  6. Loading and transport. Larger jobs, especially ones involving an attic, garage or shed as well as the main house, sometimes need more than one van run.
  7. Sorting and responsible disposal. Materials are separated for recycling and donation rather than treated as one mixed load, with genuine waste going to a licensed facility.
  8. A final walkthrough. Confirming the property is cleared to the agreed standard before the job is signed off.

Dublin vs Rural Ireland: What Actually Differs

The core process of a house clearance doesn't change much between a Dublin terrace and a farmhouse outside Carlow town — sorting, removal, loading, disposal. What changes is everything around that process, and it's worth understanding if you're trying to picture how a clearance in your own area will actually go.

In Dublin, the main practical challenges are usually access and parking rather than distance. Georgian and Victorian properties — common across the south city and parts of the northside — often have narrow hallways, steep or winding staircases, and basement-level rooms that need extra care getting furniture out safely. Terraced streets frequently have no space to park a van directly outside, which means permits, pay-and-display arrangements, or a short carry from the nearest available space. Apartment blocks add their own layer — lifts that need to be booked in advance, management companies that require notice, and loading bays with restricted hours. None of this makes a Dublin clearance harder in any absolute sense, but it does mean more coordination happens before the crew ever arrives.

Further out — in county Dublin's outer suburbs, or across Kildare, Wicklow, Kilkenny and Carlow — the pattern shifts. Properties are more likely to be detached houses, bungalows, or older farmhouses and cottages with sheds, outbuildings, and larger gardens attached, meaning the clearance can genuinely be a bigger job in terms of the ground it covers, even where access itself is easier. Distance and travel time become the bigger practical factor rather than parking — a rural property further from the base of operations means more of the day is spent travelling rather than clearing, which is one of the reasons companies typically ask for a general area before quoting, so travel time can be factored in honestly rather than guessed at.

Property age plays a role in both settings, just differently. Older Dublin properties sometimes reveal things like disused coal stores, old outbuildings, or structural quirks that need to be worked around. Older rural properties more often carry decades of general accumulation in sheds and outbuildings that were never meant to be permanent storage, simply because there was room for it. Neither is unusual — it's just useful to mention anything like this when getting a quote, so it's factored in rather than discovered on the day.

What Brings People to House Clearance in Ireland

The situations that lead someone to search for house clearance are fairly consistent across the country, even though the specifics of each one differ.

Moving house. Whether it's downsizing into somewhere smaller, moving abroad, or simply relocating, a move is one of the most common reasons for a clearance — clearing out what isn't going with you rather than trying to move it and deal with it later.

Downsizing. Often connected to moving but distinct enough to mention separately — someone moving from a family home into a smaller property, apartment, or supported accommodation, where the volume of belongings simply won't fit the next space and decisions have to be made about what's kept, sold, donated, or cleared.

Bereavement. A significant proportion of house clearance enquiries in Ireland come after someone has died, when a family or executor is left to deal with a property that's built up belongings over years or decades — often with grief and a practical deadline running at the same time. This is handled differently to a standard clearance, with time given to identify sentimental items and a pace that suits the family rather than a fixed schedule. Hoarding situations. Where belongings have built up well beyond what a normal clearance involves, often across an entire property. These jobs need a different pace and approach — more time, more care around sentimental items mixed in with genuine clutter, and no judgement about how the situation came about. See our hoarder clearance page for how that's handled.

End of tenancy. Landlords, letting agents and outgoing tenants regularly need a property cleared between one tenancy and the next, or ahead of a sale — sometimes with a tight turnaround attached. Our end-of-tenancy clearance service covers exactly this handback situation.

Business and commercial property. Not every clearance is residential — office closures, warehouse units being handed back at lease end, and commercial premises being cleared for a relocation all follow a similar underlying process, just with business deadlines and lease conditions attached rather than a personal timeline.

Getting Ready for a House Clearance

You don't need to sort through everything before a clearance company arrives — that's part of the job, wherever in Ireland it's happening. A few things genuinely help, though. Set anything you're definitely keeping to one side, somewhere clearly separate from what's being cleared, so there's no ambiguity on the day. If specific items need to be found and kept — documents, jewellery, photographs, anything sentimental — flag them when you book rather than mentioning it as the crew is loading the van, so there's time to look properly. And if access is in any way unusual — no parking directly outside, a narrow lane, a lift that needs booking in a building, a locked gate or outbuilding — mention it upfront. It means whoever turns up arrives with the right approach already worked out, rather than figuring it out on the doorstep.

It's also worth mentioning anything that might fall under hazardous waste at the same time — old paint, chemicals, a gas bottle, anything of that kind — so it can be planned into the job properly rather than discovered partway through.
