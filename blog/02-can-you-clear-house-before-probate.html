Can You Clear a House Before Probate Is Granted?

This is a general guide, not legal advice — every estate is different, and if there's any doubt, a solicitor handling the probate can give a definitive answer for your specific situation.

Probate is a widely-used term, but not everyone understands exactly what it covers when it comes to clearing a deceased person's home. It's a common worry for families: can we start clearing the house, or do we need to wait for probate to be formally granted first?

What Probate Actually Covers

Probate is the legal process of confirming a will (or, where there's no will, appointing an administrator) and giving the executor or administrator the authority to deal with the estate — including selling property, closing accounts, and distributing assets. It's specifically about legal authority over the estate's affairs, not a blanket ban on anyone entering or tidying the property.

What the Law Society Says About the Period Before a Grant

In Ireland, probate is applied for through the Probate Office, and the document that results is called a grant of representation (a grant of probate where there is a will and a named executor). The Law Society of Ireland explains that a grant is the document that allows the personal representatives to collect the assets of the person who has died and administer the estate, and that until the Probate Office issues one, the personal representatives are generally unable to deal with the assets. It also notes that in limited circumstances it may be possible to administer an estate without a grant, and that personal representatives can be personally liable if they distribute wrongly. The Law Society says estates commonly take upwards of three months to reach the grant stage. You can read this on its administration of estates page.

What that means for a house full of belongings is a question for the solicitor handling the estate, not for a clearance company. We will not tell you what your authority is. What we can tell you is how to approach it safely: treat anything that might be part of the estate as not yours to dispose of until you have been told you can, and keep a record of what you do.

What's Generally Fine Before Probate Is Granted

  • Securing the property — making sure it's locked, insured, and safe.
  • Removing perishables — clearing the fridge, bins, anything that would otherwise create a problem.
  • Sorting personal effects that clearly have no bearing on the estate's value — photos, personal papers, everyday items.
  • General tidying that doesn't involve disposing of anything of significant value.

Where It's Worth Being Careful

The main thing to be cautious about is anything that could be considered part of the estate's assets — items of financial or sentimental value that other beneficiaries might have a claim to, or anything that could affect the valuation of the estate for probate purposes. If there's more than one beneficiary, or the will is contested or unclear, it's worth getting agreement from everyone involved (and ideally a nod from the solicitor) before a full clearance goes ahead.

Keep an Inventory Before Anything Moves

Whatever stage the estate is at, a simple written inventory protects everyone. It does not need to be elaborate:

  • Walk through each room and list the main items, and photograph them.
  • Note anything of obvious value: jewellery, watches, coins, art, antiques, musical instruments, collectables.
  • Note anything of sentimental value that a family member has asked for.
  • Record the date, and who was there.
  • Keep the list with the executor or the solicitor.

Valuables, Documents and Personal Items

The things that matter most in an estate are often small, and are often not where you would expect them. Before a clearance, look for wills and legal papers, deeds, insurance and pension documents, bank and savings records, passports and certificates, jewellery, cash, photographs, and phones and computers that may hold accounts. Keep devices safe and do not wipe them until the family knows what is on them. We go through this in more detail in our guide for families, clearing a loved one's home. Questions Executors Should Ask

Before arranging a clearance, it is worth asking:

  1. The solicitor: Can anything be cleared now, and what should wait? Is there anything that should be valued first? Is there any instruction on how to record what is removed?
  2. The beneficiaries: Does everyone agree on what is to be kept, shared or cleared? Has everyone who wants to had the chance to look through the house?
  3. The insurer: Does the home insurance still apply to an empty or unattended property?
  4. The landlord, if rented: Is there a date by which the property must be returned?
  5. The clearance company: Do you hold a waste collection permit and insurance? Can items be set aside for the family? Can you work to the solicitor's timing? See our guide to choosing a house clearance company.

Timing: Before or After the Grant?

There are a few common patterns:

  • Urgent items only before the grant: securing the property, clearing perishables and removing obvious rubbish, with everything else left in place.
  • Staged clearance: clearing one part of the house (an attic, garage or shed with nothing of value) while the rest waits.
  • Full clearance after the grant: done once the solicitor and the beneficiaries agree, often ahead of a sale or a letting.
  • Full clearance before the grant, with the solicitor's agreement: sometimes needed because of a tenancy deadline, a sale or the cost of keeping an empty property secure. Where this is done, an inventory and photographs are particularly important.

Which applies to you is for the solicitor and the beneficiaries to decide. We work to whatever you decide, and we are happy to wait. For rented homes, ask the landlord early about any deadline. A Practical Middle Ground

In practice, most families find a middle ground: securing the property and clearing anything urgent straight away, then arranging the fuller clearance once probate is granted or at least once things are clearer between beneficiaries. If you're not sure where your situation sits, a quick conversation with the solicitor handling the estate is worth having before anything is disposed of.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an executor clear a house before probate is granted?

That depends on the estate, and it is a question for the solicitor handling it. The Law Society says that until a grant issues, personal representatives are generally unable to deal with the deceased's assets, so ask before disposing of anything of value. Securing the property and clearing perishables are usually the first steps.

Do I need the beneficiaries to agree first?

Where there is more than one beneficiary, it is wise to have everyone's agreement on what will be cleared and what will be kept, and ideally the solicitor's too.

Can you work with the solicitor?

Yes. We regularly coordinate with executors and solicitors on timing, access and any instructions for the property.

Can I get a quote before probate is granted?

Yes. A quote is not a booking. Photographs are often enough, and there is no obligation.

What about a rented home?

Tell the landlord or housing authority early, as there may be a deadline for returning the keys. Tell us the date and we will plan around it.
