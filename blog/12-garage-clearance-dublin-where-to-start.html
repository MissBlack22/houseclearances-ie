Garage Clearance in Dublin: Where to Even Start

A garage is where a house puts everything it hasn't decided about. Boxes from a move three houses ago, half-tins of paint, the exercise bike, tools that belonged to somebody else, and furniture that was "just going in there for now." The reason people stall on clearing one isn't laziness — it's that a garage is genuinely harder than a room, because a good chunk of what's in it can't simply go in a bin bag.

Decide What the Space Is For Before You Sort Anything

This sounds like a soft first step, but it's the one that makes every later decision fast. "Clear the garage" is a vague goal and vague goals stall. "Fit a car in," "set up a workbench," or "empty it completely before we sell" are specific, and each one gives you a different answer about what stays. If the aim is parking, anything that can't live on a wall or a shelf has to go. If it's a workshop, the tools stay and the storage boxes don't. Pick the outcome first and the sorting stops being a series of individual agonising decisions.

Sort Into Four Piles, Not Two

Keep and bin isn't enough for a garage. You want four: keep, donate or sell, general waste, and special handling — the last being anything that needs its own disposal route. That fourth pile is what catches people out, and it's usually bigger than expected. Do a first pass just pulling those items out and putting them somewhere safe, because once they're separated the rest of the garage clears at a normal pace.

What Can't Go in a Bin or a Skip

Paint, solvents, weed killer, engine oil, aerosols, and garden chemicals are household hazardous waste, and they don't belong in a household bin or a skip. Dublin's civic amenity sites do accept household hazardous waste — Dublin City Council, South Dublin County Council and Fingal County Council all take it at their recycling centres — but a charge applies for it, and the rates and accepted lists differ between councils, so it's worth checking your own council's current page before you load the car. Leftover usable paint is a separate case: the Rediscovery Centre runs a paint reuse scheme with donation points around Dublin, so tins still in good condition don't have to be treated as waste at all.

Electricals, Batteries and Tyres Each Have a Free Route

The old fridge, the dead strimmer, the box of cables — all of that is WEEE, and household electrical waste is accepted free of charge at civic amenity sites and recycling centres across Ireland. Retailers also have to take back an old item free on a one-for-one, like-for-like basis when you buy a replacement, and larger electrical shops must accept small appliances without any purchase at all.

Batteries are similar: any shop selling batteries must accept waste batteries back free of charge, and that includes car batteries at outlets selling them. Tyres go through Circol ELT, the national end-of-life tyre scheme — the usual route is a participating tyre retailer, and their site lists members. None of these three need to cost you anything, which matters when the hazardous pile already will.

Gas Cylinders Go Back to the Supplier

Patio gas and butane cylinders are the one thing never to guess about. They should not go into a bin or a skip under any circumstances, because a crushed cylinder can explode. The standard route is returning them to the supplier or a stockist for that brand — Calor cylinders back to a Calor retailer, and so on. Some recycling centres accept them and some don't, so ring ahead rather than arriving with one in the boot.

What's Left Is Usually Volume, Not Difficulty

Once the hazardous and special-handling items are out, what remains in most garages is bulk: old furniture, timber offcuts, broken storage units, bags of unidentifiable soft goods, and rubble from a job that finished years ago. It's not complicated, it's just heavy and there's a lot of it — and that's the point where car boot runs to the recycling centre stop making sense.
